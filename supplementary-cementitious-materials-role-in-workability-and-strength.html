Supplementary Cementitious Materials'' Role in Workability and Strength

supplementary-cementitious-materialsworkability-improvementfly-ashground-granulated-blast-furnace-slagconcrete-properties
Created 2026-09-09 · Updated 2026-09-09

Supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) are finely powdered mineral admixtures—often natural minerals or industrial by‑products such as fly ash, ground‑granulated blast‑furnace slag, and silica fume—that are introduced to a concrete mix before or during mixing to modify its fresh and hardened behavior【ref-1】. Their fine particle morphology (spherical fly ash, glassy slag, ultra‑fine silica fume) acts as a microscopic lubricant that can lower water demand and improve flow, while the siliceous or siliceous‑aluminous chemistry enables a moisture‑driven pozzolanic reaction with calcium hydroxide to generate additional cementitious phases that contribute to later‑age strength【ref-1】. Consequently, SCMs simultaneously enhance workability and strengthen the hardened matrix, provided dosage thresholds (e.g., silica fume below about five percent of cement mass) and environmental conditions such as hot weather are respected【ref-1】.

Material purpose and applicability

Supplementary cementitious materials are powdered mineral admixtures, typically derived from natural sources or industrial by‑products, that are introduced to the concrete mix before or during mixing. By virtue of their fine particle size they modify the plastic (workability) and hardened (strength) characteristics of Portland cement concrete, thereby enhancing the performance of pavement structures. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 331)

Composition and characteristics

Supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) are defined by their constituents—fly ash, ground‑granulated blast‑furnace slag (GGBFS), silica fume, and other pozzolanic powders—that are either natural minerals or industrial by‑products. Their composition is primarily siliceous or siliceous‑and‑aluminous, and they are supplied as a finely divided, powdered material that can be added before or during mixing. The structure of these materials is characterized by very fine particles; fly ash and GGBFS exhibit a fine spherical shape and a glassy surface, while silica fume possesses an extremely high specific surface area. These particle‑size, shape, and surface‑texture attributes govern the fresh‑state behavior: the spherical particles promote flow and reduce water demand, whereas the high surface area of silica fume raises water demand and stickiness when used above about five percent by cement mass. In addition to physical effects, the reactive chemistry of siliceous/aluminous powders enables them, in the presence of moisture, to chemically react with calcium hydroxide at ordinary temperatures, forming new cementitious compounds that contribute to later‑age strength. Together, the fine powdery nature, specific mineral composition, and surface characteristics define how SCMs modify concrete workability and enhance hardened‑state performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151), Ch. 10 (pp. 326–331)

Variations in the type and amount of SCMs change concrete workability through their particle characteristics. Fly ash and GGBF slag generally enhance flow because fly ash particles are fine and spherical while slag presents a glassy surface, yet certain fly ashes can stiffen early when used in hot weather. Silica fume behaves differently with dosage: at less than five percent of cement mass its spherical particles help separate grains and improve workability, but above that threshold the very high surface area sharply raises water demand and makes the mix sticky. These compositional and proportioning effects illustrate how the physical form of each SCM governs the balance between ease of placement and the need for additional water or admixtures. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151)

Key material properties

The workability contribution of SCMs is governed primarily by their physical characteristics: fly ash particles are fine and spherical, while GGBF slag particles have a smooth glassy surface, both of which lubricate the mix and allow lower water content. Silica fume, in contrast, possesses an extremely high specific surface area; at dosages below five percent its roughly spherical particles can aid particle separation, but above that level the excessive surface area raises water demand and makes the paste sticky. Consequently, particle size, shape, surface texture and dosage thresholds are the key properties influencing the balance between improved workability and strength development. The performance of pozzolanic supplementary cementitious materials hinges on several key attributes: they must be siliceous or siliceous‑and‑aluminous in composition, exist as a finely divided powder to provide ample surface area, and be capable of chemically reacting with calcium hydroxide when moisture is present. This reaction occurs at ordinary temperatures, generating additional cementitious compounds that contribute to strength development while allowing the mix to remain workable. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151), Ch. 10 (p. 326)

The workability of supplementary cementitious materials is characterized by their particle morphology and surface texture as defined in the relevant material specifications—for example, fly ash is classified under AASHTO M 295/ASTM C618 and its fine spherical particles, together with the glassy surfaces of GGBF slag, are associated with improved flow. Silica fume’s very high surface area raises water demand when used above about five percent of cement mass, while at lower dosages its roughly spherical particles can actually enhance workability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151)

Behavior and mechanisms

Supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash and ground‑granulated blast‑furnace slag typically increase concrete workability, the fine spherical particles of fly ash and the glassy surface of slag acting like lubricants that allow lower water content without sacrificing flow. Under elevated temperatures, however, certain fly ashes can stiffen early, leading to a loss of workability. Silica fume behaves dose‑dependently: at less than about five percent of cement mass its spherical particles help separate cement grains and can improve workability, whereas higher dosages raise water demand and make the mix sticky. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151)

The workability impact of SCMs is governed primarily by their physical characteristics: fine, spherical fly‑ash particles act like tiny ball bearings that lubricate the mix, while the glassy surface of GGBF slag reduces interparticle friction and promotes flow. However, silica fume will markedly increase the water demand and stickiness at dosages above five percent by mass of cement because of the high surface area, limiting its beneficial effect on workability. Strength development is controlled by a moisture‑driven pozzolanic reaction: its large surface area allows it, in the presence of moisture, to react with calcium hydroxide generated during cement hydration at ordinary temperatures, producing additional cementitious compounds that contribute to strength development. Consequently, particle size, shape and surface texture (physical), the high specific surface area coupled with the chemical pozzolanic reaction (chemical), and the ball‑bearing lubrication effect (mechanical) together dictate how SCMs modify concrete workability and strength. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151), Ch. 10 (p. 326)

Durability and deterioration

Although fly ash and GGBF slag typically enhance concrete workability through their fine, spherical or glassy particles, their benefits can be compromised by specific deterioration mechanisms. In hot weather some fly ashes may cause early stiffening and loss of workability, while silica fume dosages above five percent by mass of cement markedly increase water demand and mixture stickiness, both of which undermine the intended improvements in workability and consequently affect strength development if not properly managed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151)

Material interactions and compatibility

Fly ash and ground‑granulated blast‑furnace slag interact with the cement binder primarily as microscopic lubricants; their fine, spherical (fly ash) or glassy (slag) particles reduce inter‑particle friction among cement grains, lower the paste volume required to coat aggregates, and thereby improve workability. By decreasing aggregate coating demand they also lessen overall water consumption, which supports strength development without compromising durability. In contrast, silica fume’s extremely high surface area draws water from the mix, so at dosages above about five percent it raises water demand and makes the paste sticky; however, when used below that threshold its spherical particles can help separate cement grains and modestly boost workability. Chemically, pozzolanic SCMs—siliceous or siliceous‑aluminous in nature—do not act as primary binders but react with calcium hydroxide liberated during cement hydration. This pozzolanic reaction generates additional C‑S‑H and related phases that reinforce the matrix, enhancing long‑term strength while the physical particle effects govern early‑age workability and aggregate interaction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151), Ch. 10 (p. 326)

Selection, specification, and improvement

Selection of an SCM is governed first by its particle morphology: the fine spherical nature of fly ash and the glassy surface of GGBF slag particles both tend to boost workability, whereas Silica fume will markedly increase the water demand and stickiness at dosages above five percent by mass of cement. Designers also evaluate chemical makeup; a pozzolanic SCM must be a siliceous or siliceous and aluminous material that possesses little or no intrinsic cementitious value, is finely divided, and can react with calcium hydroxide in the presence of moisture at ordinary temperatures. Additional practical criteria include environmental exposure—hot‑weather conditions may cause some fly ashes to stiffen early, prompting selection of a more stable ash or an alternative SCM—and the intended structural application, such as avoiding silica fume in pavement concrete despite any workability advantage. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151), Ch. 10 (p. 326)

Using fine‑spherical fly ash and the glassy particles of ground‑granulated blast‑furnace slag is a proven way to boost concrete workability, so specifying these SCMs according to AASHTO M 295/ASTM C618 helps maintain flow while preserving strength. In hot weather, selecting fly ashes that do not exhibit early stiffening—or substituting with GGBF slag—mitigates the loss of workability noted for some ash types. Silica fume should be limited to less than five percent by cement mass; at lower dosages its spherical particles can aid grain separation, whereas higher levels markedly raise water demand and stickiness. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151)

Performance evaluation and implications

The fine spherical particles of fly ash and the glassy surface of ground‑granulated blast‑furnace slag improve concrete workability, enabling easier placement and potentially lower water content during pavement construction; however, in hot weather certain fly ashes can stiffen early, reducing workability and risking a poorer surface finish that may affect long‑term durability. Silica fume can modestly aid workability at low dosages but sharply raises water demand above about five percent by cement mass and is therefore seldom used in pavement mixes. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 151)

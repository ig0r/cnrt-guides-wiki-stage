Pavement Types

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Pavement Types is a topic under Pavement Design.
concrete-pavementjpcpjrcpcrcppavement-preservation
Created 2026-09-01 · Updated 2026-09-02

Pavement types are categories of road surfaces distinguished by their material composition and structural characteristics, classified according to their structural form and reinforcement strategy. Concrete pavements utilize specific design approaches such as jointed plain systems that control shrinkage cracks through transverse joints, continuously reinforced systems that manage cracking via longitudinal steel, thin slabs sized for wheel loads to limit tensile stresses, and modular precast elements assembled on site [1]. These distinct configurations determine how the pavement structure distributes traffic loads and manages internal stresses to preserve slab integrity and durability over time.

Concrete Pavement Classification and System Types

Concrete pavements are categorized by their structural form and reinforcement strategy, primarily including jointed plain, continuously reinforced, thin-slab, and modular systems [1]. Jointed plain concrete relies on a network of transverse joints to control shrinkage cracking and preserve slab integrity [1]. Continuously reinforced concrete eliminates transverse joints by using longitudinal steel reinforcement to manage cracking at predictable spacings [1]. Thin concrete pavements utilize shallow slabs designed to limit tensile stresses under wheel loads without extensive reinforcement, while modular systems employ precast elements for rapid construction and durability [1].

Plan and profile views of a continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP) section. [1]

The figure displays plan and profile views of a concrete slab containing longitudinal reinforcing steel bars positioned near the center of the depth.

Research problem — The selection of concrete pavement types involves complex trade-offs between construction efficiency, structural durability, and environmental impact, particularly regarding the need to minimize traffic disruptions and life-cycle emissions [1]. Inadequate jointing strategies in plain concrete pavements lead to uncontrolled cracking and reduced service life, necessitating alternative designs such as continuously reinforced systems or optimized thin-pavement geometries to manage stresses and maintain integrity [1]. The use of locally sourced aggregates that do not meet conventional quality standards introduces uncertainty regarding the strength and durability of concrete pavements, highlighting a gap in material specification for sustainable construction practices [1].

The following figure illustrates corner cracking in jointed concrete pavement.

Corner cracking in jointed concrete pavement [1]

The image displays a section of rigid pavement characterized by intersecting transverse and longitudinal joints that divide the surface into rectangular slabs. Visible structural distress manifests as corner cracking, where fractures originate at the slab corners and propagate inward across the concrete surface.

State of the art — The current practice for concrete pavements is dominated by Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) and Continuously Reinforced Concrete Pavement (CRCP), with CRCP serving as the leading choice for high-traffic urban corridors due to its joint-free design that minimizes maintenance [1]. Emerging alternatives include modular systems utilizing precast components for rapid construction and repair, as well as Thin Concrete Pavement (TCP) which optimizes slab thickness based on truck wheel geometry to reduce stresses, though TCP adoption remains limited in the United States [1].

Novelty & rigor — The approach introduces a distinctive classification system that optimizes slab geometry to ensure only one wheel set contacts each individual panel, thereby enabling the use of significantly thinner concrete sections [1]. This method is reproducible through specific design protocols that mandate thin profiles and compact dimensions, which inherently reduce curling stresses and improve joint load transfer compared to conventional large-slab systems [1].

The following figure illustrates a next-generation concrete surface (NGCS) that embodies these design principles.

Next-generation concrete surface (NGCS) with a pedestrian walking on it. [1]

The image displays a close-up view of a concrete pavement featuring closely spaced, parallel grooves running horizontally across the frame. A person’s legs and feet are visible in the background, casting a shadow on the textured surface under bright sunlight. This specific pavement configuration is identified as next-generation concrete surface (NGCS), which combines grinding and grooving to reduce noise from tire-pavement interaction while increasing friction.

Implications — Designers should prioritize modular concrete systems, such as precast slabs and interlocking pavers, for projects requiring rapid installation or minimal traffic disruption [1]. Specifications ought to incorporate reduced cement content with supplementary materials in these modular applications to maximize sustainability benefits while maintaining durability through factory-controlled quality [1].

The figure below illustrates a permeable interlocking concrete pavement system.

Cross-section of a permeable interlocking concrete pavement system. [1]

The figure displays the stratified layers of a modular pavement structure, consisting of pavers at the surface, followed by bedding and granular base layers over a subgrade.

Limitations — The sustainability benefits of modular concrete systems depend on achieving long-term durability and effectively reducing cement content through supplementary materials [1]. These systems are generally restricted to specific contexts, such as precast slabs for short construction windows or interlocking pavers for low-speed urban settings, limiting their applicability across all roadway types [1].

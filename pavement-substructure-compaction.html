Pavement Substructure Compaction

pavement-substructurecompactionuniform-supportsettlementsubgrade-treatment
Created 2026-09-09 · Updated 2026-09-09

Pavement substructure compaction is the process of densifying the subgrade and base layers to provide a uniform, stiff support for concrete pavements, thereby preventing settlement‑related distress such as slab deflection, transverse or diagonal cracking, and heave[1]. It involves preparing the in‑situ soils—classifying them, scarifying or blending where needed, and stabilizing low‑density, high‑plasticity materials—and then compacting at the optimum moisture content with appropriate rollers and a prescribed number of passes, verified by field moisture‑and‑density testing and stiffness measurements (K or Mr) through plate‑load tests[1]. Continuous monitoring, proof rolling to locate soft spots, and remedial actions such as additional compaction or material replacement ensure that the achieved stiffness is uniform across the pavement footprint[1].

Purpose and applicability

The primary problem solved by pavement substructure compaction is the settlement‑related distress that arises when the underlying soils or base layers are insufficiently compacted. As stated, “Adequate compaction of the subgrade and base eliminates settlement and uneven support that would otherwise increase slab deflection, raise bending stresses, and cause transverse or diagonal cracking in concrete pavements.” Moreover, “Lack of uniform and adequate compaction during initial construction is one of the most frequent causes of settlement in concrete pavements.” The method therefore “eliminates the problem of inadequate compaction that would otherwise produce soft spots in the subgrade or base course and lead to settlement, heaving, or cracking.” [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 108–311)

When the in‑situ subgrade consists of uniform soils, “compact[ing] the subgrade soil at the optimum moisture content using the proper rolling equipment and number of passes determined by test strips” is appropriate; field density testing (M&D based on the Proctor curve) then provides the stiffness assumed in design. “Close monitoring of the moisture and in‑place stiffness is required in all cases,” and “proof rolling of the subgrade and base course will identify areas of inadequate compaction.” If a soft zone is detected, additional compaction should be attempted. However, “With poorer soils that have low density (below 100) and a high plastic index, achieving the best compaction possible through proper M&D still results in poor strength,” so the project must shift to soil modification (e.g., cement or fly‑ash stabilization) or removal and replacement with improved material, using stiffness‑based acceptance criteria such as K or resilient modulus. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 303–307)

Required inputs

To obtain adequate substructure compaction the subgrade and base layers must be compact the subgrade soil at the optimum moisture content using the proper rolling equipment and number of passes determined by test strips so that density meets or exceeds the Proctor target. When native soils are weak—exhibiting a high plastic index or low density—they require soil modification with cement, fly ash, etc. to achieve the design stiffness and uniform support. The prepared subgrade must be such that subgrade should either be scarified and blended when built directly on natural soils, or an unbound aggregate base constructed to provide a consistent platform. These actions satisfy the required modulus of subgrade reaction (K) or resilient modulus (Mr), ensure uniform support and drainage, and prevent settlement and heave cracking. [1]

Design for substructure compaction requires that the subgrade be compacted at its optimum moisture content using the appropriate rollers and a number of passes defined by test‑strip results, with field moisture‑and‑density (M&D) testing—based on the standard or modified Proctor—to verify that the target density is achieved. Acceptance is tied to meeting the prescribed stiffness values, expressed as a modulus of subgrade reaction (K) or resilient modulus (Mr), which are confirmed by plate‑load tests performed either statically for K or cyclically for Mr. During construction the moisture content and in‑place stiffness must be continuously monitored, and proof rolling is used to locate any soft spots; identified zones are remedied with additional compaction passes or, if necessary, excavation and replacement with a better‑graded fill. The design also calls for either scarifying and blending the natural‑soil subgrade to reduce differential volume changes or placing an unbound aggregate base that provides a uniform support platform before concrete placement. [1]

Work sequence

Before any pavement substructure compaction can begin, the in‑situ subgrade must be fully prepared and characterized. First, “The soil classification and engineering properties of the in situ subgrade soils are generally determined during the project feasibility stage” and incorporated into the design to set stiffness and support criteria. Next, the natural‑soil subgrade is treated to provide a uniform platform—either by scarifying and blending the soil to reduce differential volume changes or by constructing an unbound aggregate base; as stated, “the subgrade should either be scarified and blended to try to minimize differential volume changes”. After this preparation, construction crews must perform quality‑control checks: “Close monitoring of the moisture and in-place stiffness is required” and a proof‑rolling survey—“proof rolling of the subgrade and base course will identify areas of inadequate compaction”. Any soft spots identified are repaired by additional compaction or material replacement before the main compaction operations commence. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 303–311)

  1. Classify subgrade soils and determine engineering properties – “The soil classification and engineering properties of the in situ subgrade soils are generally determined during the project feasibility stage.”
  2. Identify optimum moisture content – Conduct field moisture‑and‑density (M&D) testing based on a standard or modified Proctor test: “field moisture and density (referred to as M&D) is based on the standard or modified Proctor density test, which relates compacted density to the moisture state of the soil and compactive effort.”
  3. Monitor moisture and in‑place stiffness during construction – “Close monitoring of the moisture and in-place stiffness is required in all cases.”
  4. Compact subgrade at optimum moisture – Use appropriate roller equipment and the number of passes prescribed by test strips: “compact[ing] the subgrade soil at the optimum moisture content using the proper rolling equipment and number of passes determined by test strips.”
    - If the native soil is low‑density and highly plastic, stabilize it first with cement, fly ash, or similar modifiers: “To be able to use these poorer soils, it will take soil modification with cement, fly ash, etc.”
  5. Proof roll prepared layers – Perform proof rolling of the subgrade and base course to locate soft spots: “proof rolling of the subgrade and base course will identify areas of inadequate compaction.”
  6. Repair identified soft areas – Apply additional compaction passes, or if density cannot be achieved, excavate and replace with improved fill: “These “soft” areas should be repaired by additional compaction or, if necessary, removed and replaced with improved materials.” [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 303–307)

Staging and logistics

Limited space around culverts, utility patches, or other confined zones makes it difficult to achieve uniform high‑level compaction of subgrade and base materials, increasing the risk of settlement cracking. Environmental conditions such as a sufficiently cold climate that allows freezing temperatures to reach the subgrade, combined with moisture in the freezing zone and susceptible low‑plasticity silts, promote frost heave that undermines support. Likewise, soils that swell and shrink with moisture changes—typically heavy clays—create volume instability, while erodible base types can be pumped out under traffic, further reducing support for the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 108–109)

Quality control and acceptance

Inspectors should verify that the substructure has been compacted to an adequate level and that support is uniform across the area, looking for variability in subgrade material properties or segregation of granular base course material. They must assess moisture‑density conditions by conducting standard or modified Proctor tests at optimum moisture content with the prescribed rollers and pass count. The key acceptance criteria are not a single density number but the achieved stiffness—expressed as modulus of subgrade reaction (K) from static plate‑load testing or resilient modulus (Mr) from cyclic testing—and its uniformity. Continuous monitoring of in‑place moisture and stiffness, together with proof rolling of the subgrade and base course, is required to locate any soft spots; identified areas must be re‑compacted or replaced before construction proceeds. Additional checks include evaluating the base for susceptibility to pumping or erosion and confirming that the subgrade does not present volume‑stability issues such as swelling soils or frost‑heave potential. [1]

Expected performance and maintenance

Insufficient or non‑uniform compaction of the subgrade and base is a primary source of pavement distress. When the compacted layer does not meet design density, the underlying foundation deforms over time, producing settlement that manifests as cracking in the concrete slab. As the report states, “settlement of the slab occurs and the resulting increase in deflection produces transverse or diagonal cracks.” This settlement is most often traced to poor construction practices; indeed, “Lack of uniform and adequate compaction during initial construction is one of the most frequent causes of settlement in concrete pavements.” In addition to pure compression loss, an erodible base can be washed out under traffic loading, creating unsupported zones that generate high bending stresses and localized cracking—“Base erodibility and loss of support.” Together these mechanisms lead to transverse, diagonal, and heave cracks, as well as long‑term deformation of the pavement. [1]

Preventive maintenance begins with preparing the existing natural‑soil subgrade – it should be scarified and blended, or an unbound aggregate base constructed, to provide a uniform support platform that “to prevent settlement and heave cracks.” During construction crews must achieve uniform compaction by “compact the subgrade soil at the optimum moisture content using the proper rolling equipment and number of passes determined by test strips.” Continuous quality control requires “Close monitoring of the moisture and in-place stiffness is required,” which is carried out with field‑density and moisture testing (Proctor methods) and, where needed, plate‑load testing to verify that the modulus of subgrade reaction or resilient modulus meets design values. After each lift, “proof rolling of the subgrade and base course will identify areas of inadequate compaction.” When a soft zone is detected, it is first treated with additional compaction; if acceptable density cannot be achieved, “soft areas should be repaired by additional compaction or, if necessary, removed and replaced with improved materials,” and low‑density or high‑plasticity soils may be stabilized with cement, fly ash, or similar modifiers before final compaction. If acceptance criteria are still not met, remedial soil stabilization or reconstruction is required. [1]

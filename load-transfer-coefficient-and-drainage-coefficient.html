Load Transfer Coefficient and Drainage Coefficient

aashto-guideunbonded-overlayscritical-design-variablestrafficload-transfer-coefficient
Created 2026-09-09 · Updated 2026-09-09

The load transfer coefficient (J) and drainage coefficient (Cd) are critical input parameters in the design of bonded or unbonded concrete overlays, quantifying respectively how effectively traffic loads are transferred across joints and how efficiently moisture is removed through the underlying pavement layers [1]. By incorporating these coefficients into the AASHTO overlay design procedure, engineers can determine a safe, serviceable overlay thickness, decide whether pre‑overlay repairs are required, and avoid unnecessary over‑design that would lead to premature distress [1].

Purpose and design objectives

The load transfer coefficient (J) and drainage coefficient (Cd) are incorporated into overlay design to remedy deficiencies in an existing pavement’s ability to distribute traffic loads and shed water; by quantifying how effectively loads are transferred across joints and how efficiently moisture is removed, designers can determine a safe, serviceable thickness for unbonded overlays, decide whether pre‑overlay repairs are required, and avoid unnecessary over‑design that would lead to premature distress. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 25–32)

Scope and applicability

The load‑transfer coefficient (J) and drainage coefficient (C d) are applied when designing bonded concrete overlays on existing pavements. Critical design variables that have a moderate to high impact on the thickness design of unbonded overlays over concrete and composite pavements include … load transfer (J), drainage coefficient (C d) .... The most sensitive variables for this method when designing unbonded overlays over composite pavements include traffic, load transfer coefficient, drainage coefficient, modulus of rupture, k‑value, and serviceability loss. Designing unbonded overlays over asphalt pavements also treats the load transfer and drainage coefficients as critical variables influencing overlay performance. [1]

When the existing pavement exhibits significant load‑transfer or drainage problems, relying on the load transfer coefficient and drainage coefficient alone can lead to an overly thick concrete overlay. In such cases the guide recommends performing pre‑overlay repairs to correct those deficiencies before placing a bonded overlay, effectively shifting the design approach away from using the coefficients as primary inputs. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (p. 25)

Design inputs and requirements

The design of the load‑transfer coefficient (J) and the drainage coefficient (C d ) for unbonded overlays is governed by the procedures set out in the 1993 AASHTO Guide, which treats the overlay as a new pavement structure. Within that guide, the presence of dowel bars at joints is required to adjust J, typically reducing it from 4.4 to 3.2, and any drainage improvements together with an accurate estimate of mean concrete strength are used to define the appropriate C d value. Traffic volume and support conditions are also treated as fixed project constraints for a given job. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 31–32)

Design methods and criteria

The 1993 AASHTO Guide requires that an unbonded overlay be sized with the same structural design procedure used for a new pavement, treating the load‑transfer coefficient (J) and drainage coefficient (C d ) as key input parameters. Dowel bars at joints modify J—typically reducing it from about 4.4 to 3.2—and any drainage improvements alter C d, both of which directly affect the calculated overlay thickness within that standard design framework. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 31–32)

The design procedure requires that both the load‑transfer coefficient (J) and the drainage coefficient (Cd) be judged based on the condition of the existing pavement, since their values reflect how effectively loads are transferred and water is drained through the underlying layers. When the evaluation shows a major deficiency in either parameter, the recommended practice is to carry out pre‑overlay repairs before installing the bonded concrete overlay so that the overlay thickness is not over‑designed. The guide does not prescribe specific numerical thresholds, safety factors, or reliability limits for these coefficients; compliance is achieved by ensuring they are adequate for the observed pavement condition. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (p. 25)

Structural details and interfaces

The 1993 AASHTO overlay design requires provision of dowel bars at all joint locations, because their presence directly modifies the load‑transfer coefficient and therefore the required overlay thickness. In addition, the design calls for drainage improvements—such as adequate surface slope or sub‑drainage features—to achieve a suitable drainage coefficient that influences the thickness calculation. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 31–32)

The design of the interface between an existing pavement and a bonded concrete overlay must ensure that both load transfer and drainage at the joint are adequate for the prevailing pavement condition; if the existing surface exhibits significant deficiencies in either, pre‑overlay repairs are required to improve those properties before the overlay is placed, thereby avoiding unnecessary increase in overlay thickness. [1]

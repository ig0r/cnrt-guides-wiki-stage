Recycled Concrete Aggregate Utilization

recycled-concretepavement-constructionsustainabilityrecyclingenvironmental-benefits
Created 2026-09-09 · Updated 2026-09-09

Recycled concrete aggregate utilization is a decision‑making framework that evaluates whether to incorporate RCA into a pavement project by comparing sustainability trade‑offs such as transport distance, additional cement requirements, life‑cycle cost and opportunity cost against conventional aggregate options [1][4]. It directs the placement of RCA—most often in the base, subbase, or the thick lower lift of a two‑lift composite concrete pavement—to achieve lower material and transportation costs, reduced carbon footprint and waste while still meeting required performance criteria [1][2][4]. Because RCA is treated as an engineered material, its physical and chemical properties must be characterized before mix‑design adjustments are made, ensuring that environmental benefits do not compromise strength, durability or safety [1][2].

Purpose and decision context

Recycled concrete aggregate utilization is intended to support the decision of which aggregate source to use in a pavement project—whether to incorporate RCA into the new concrete mix or to select a locally sourced conventional aggregate—by evaluating sustainability trade‑offs such as transport distance, additional cement requirements, life‑cycle cost and opportunity cost. It also supports the decision of selecting a concrete pavement design that deliberately incorporates RCA—such as using it in the base and subbase layers or in the lower lift of a two‑lift composite pavement—to achieve lower material and transportation costs, reduce environmental impacts, and still meet required performance criteria. The concept guides the decision to employ recycled concrete aggregate in the bottom lift of a two‑lift concrete pavement system. By selecting RCA—regardless of its quality—for this thick underlying layer, designers can meet economic criteria such as lower capital costs and longer service life, achieve environmental goals like reduced carbon footprint and waste, and address social considerations including less disruption and improved safety. [1][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [4] — Ch. 6 (p. 84)

The primary problem that recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) utilization seeks to solve is the tension between environmental sustainability objectives—such as waste reduction, lower greenhouse‑gas emissions, and reduced haul distances—and the practical demands of delivering cost‑effective, high‑performing pavements. Recycled concrete aggregate utilization tackles the need to lower pavement construction expenses and accelerate project timelines while simultaneously addressing the waste management challenge posed by deteriorated concrete structures. The utilization of recycled concrete aggregate is driven by the need to address the environmental impacts that arise from its production and use, ensuring compliance with applicable legislation and regulations. The goal is to identify and mitigate potential environmental concerns throughout a project’s lifecycle, incorporating specific strategies during both planning/design and construction phases. The utilization of recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) tackles the need to lower CO₂ emissions tied to cement production while supplying a sustainable, high‑quality aggregate for pavement work. [1][2][3][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [2] — Benefits (p. 70); [3] — Ch. 7 (p. 69); [4] — Ch. 6 (pp. 78–84)

Scope and applicability

Recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) is appropriate across a wide spectrum of pavement projects and development phases. The guides agree that RCA can be placed in any pavement layer—surface, base, or subbase—and note that “Recycled concrete aggregates (RCA) are versatile because they can be used in any pavement layer.” It is therefore suitable for both new construction and rehabilitation/reconstruction work. New‑construction contexts highlighted include rural paving jobs where the project “incorporate recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) in the new concrete mixture to be used on a rural paving project,” and two‑lift composite pavements, described as “One concrete pavement design option that is particularly well suited to incorporating recycled materials as aggregate in new concrete is two‑lift composite pavement” with “the thicker lower lift uses recycled materials and/or other less suitable local aggregates.” Rehabilitation projects can harvest the existing pavement, a situation the guides call “These options are often particularly attractive when the original pavement structure is the source of the recycled materials used in the reconstructed pavement.”

Asset conditions that favor RCA include concrete exhibiting alkali‑silica reaction or D‑cracking, because “Pavement suffering from ASR or D-cracking can be recycled instead of discarded,” and any condition where an aggregate replacement is needed. The guides stress that “recycled concrete must be viewed as another source of aggregate with characteristics and value equivalent to the aggregate material it replaces ton for ton,” making RCA suitable for unbound soil‑aggregate base courses (“AASHTO M 319, Reclaimed Concrete Aggregate for Unbound Soil-Aggregate Base Course”) and as coarse aggregate in hydraulic cement concrete (“AASHTO MP 16, Reclaimed Concrete Aggregate for Use as Coarse Aggregate in Hydraulic Cement Concrete”). Its higher surface area also enhances carbonation, as “Sequestering is more efficient when RCA is used as base material due to favorable environmental conditions in a pavement subsurface base system.”

From a project‑development perspective, suitability begins at the design stage, where mix proportions are selected and trade‑offs such as transport distance, additional cement requirements (“the inclusion of the RCA would require mixture adjustments that require an additional 50 lb of portland cement per cubic yard of concrete”), and material characterization are evaluated. Construction phases benefit when on‑site recycling facilities are available, allowing reduced material costs and faster schedules. The guides further note that “Two‑lift pavement construction can be used to satisfy the economic, environmental, and social aspects essential for sustainable design,” reinforcing RCA’s role throughout design, construction, and rehabilitation. [1][2][4]

RCA should be avoided when the logistical and material penalties outweigh its environmental advantages, when its inherent properties cannot meet performance requirements, or when it is being considered for pavement layers that demand high‑quality aggregate. The guides note that if the nearest source of recycled concrete aggregate lies far away—e.g., 100 mi versus a locally available natural aggregate only 5 mi distant—and its use would require significant mixture adjustments such as adding an extra 50 lb of Portland cement per cubic yard, the sustainability benefits can be outweighed by higher transportation emissions, added material cost, and potential performance penalties. Under those conditions a holistic trade‑off analysis that includes life‑cycle cost, greenhouse‑gas impact, and project priorities should favor the local aggregate or another alternative rather than RCA. Additionally, recycled concrete aggregate is unsuitable when its measured properties cannot satisfy the required pavement performance, such as strength or durability criteria, or when the source material’s condition is uncertain; engineering performance of the final pavement must not be compromised. The guides also agree that RCA is appropriate for the thick, low‑modulus bottom lift of a two‑lift pavement but is not appropriate for the thin, high‑performance surface layer that requires superior quality aggregate to meet strength and wear requirements; in those cases a conventional high‑grade aggregate should be used instead. [1][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [4] — Ch. 6 (p. 84)

Information needs

The guides concur that “Recycled concrete aggregate should be considered an engineered material for which the properties must be determined prior to use.” Condition data must identify “Pavement suffering from ASR or D‑cracking can be recycled instead of discarded,” and also capture environmental parameters because “The rate of sequestration varies directly with temperature, surface area, relative humidity, and concentration of CO .” Site information should verify logistics such as “the nearest source of RCA is 100 mi away while an acceptable local extracted aggregate is only 5 mi away,” and confirm the feasibility of “Construction time can be expedited with on‑site recycling plants.” Cost considerations include “Material costs are reduced,” the need for mixture adjustments – “the inclusion of the RCA would require mixture adjustments that require an additional 50 lb of portland cement per cubic yard of concrete,” and broader sustainability metrics: “Some examples of sustainability value include life-cycle cost, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, energy use, water/air quality, waste generation.” The reduction in disposal is noted as “The need for old concrete disposal is reduced.” Stakeholder input is critical; decision‑makers must weigh “considerations involve the following: priorities and values of the organization or project, pavement performance, cost, impact magnitude and duration, and risk,” and achieve “Overcoming this requires a positive attitude among the various stakeholders including the responsible transportation agency, the environmental agency in control, contractors, producers, and the public.” Finally, placement as a base layer is advantageous because “Sequestering is more efficient when RCA is used as base material due to favorable environmental conditions in a pavement subsurface base system.” [1][2][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [2] — Benefits (p. 70); [4] — Ch. 6 (p. 78)

The information used to specify recycled concrete aggregate must be sufficiently accurate to reflect the actual physical and chemical properties of the reclaimed material, because RCA is treated as an engineered component whose characteristics have to be known before mixture design or construction adjustments are made. It also needs to be current, drawing on recent guidance such as the 2018 practitioner’s reference guide, so that any changes in standards or best‑practice are captured. Finally, the data set must be complete enough to include details about the original concrete source and why it was removed from service, ensuring that all property variations are considered when evaluating suitability for base, subbase, or new concrete lifts. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 32)

Alternatives

The guides recommend that analysts compare at least two alternatives: using RCA sourced from a distant location versus employing locally extracted natural aggregate. As one example, “it may be desired to incorporate recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) in the new concrete mixture to be used on a rural paving project, but the nearest source of RCA is 100 mi away while an acceptable local extracted aggregate is only 5 mi away”. The comparison must also consider that “the inclusion of the RCA would require mixture adjustments that require an additional 50 lb of portland cement per cubic yard of concrete”.

Feasible functional‑role comparisons include: 1. “using RCA as an unbound soil‑aggregate base layer” versus the “conventional option of virgin aggregate”. 2. “incorporating RCA as a coarse aggregate in new hydraulic cement concrete pavement” versus virgin aggregate. 3. Base and subbase configurations that rely solely on recycled materials, as noted by “It is common practice to use recycled materials as aggregate in the base and subbase layers as part of a concrete pavement design.” 4. Composite designs such as the two‑lift system: “One concrete pavement design option that is particularly well suited to incorporating recycled materials as aggregate in new concrete is two-lift composite pavement.” In this scheme, “the relatively thin upper concrete lift may include abrasion-resistant and more durable aggregates, possibly shipped great distances; whereas, the thicker lower lift uses recycled materials and/or other less suitable local aggregates.” 5. Inclusion of alternative recycled aggregates alongside RCA, for example “Recycled materials, such as RCA, air‑cooled blast furnace slag (ACBFS), and even reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP), have also been used as aggregate in new concrete.”

Standards cited for the base‑layer and concrete‑lift options are “AASHTO M 319, Reclaimed Concrete Aggregate for Unbound Soil-Aggregate Base Course” and “AASHTO MP 16, Reclaimed Concrete Aggregate for Use as Coarse Aggregate in Hydraulic Cement Concrete”. The guides also stress that “recycled concrete must be viewed as another source of aggregate with characteristics and value equivalent to the aggregate material it replaces ton for ton”, and note that “The use of RCA of any quality is more than adequate for the construction of the thick underling bottom lift of the two‑lift system, including RCA mixed with RAP.” [1][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [4] — Ch. 6 (pp. 78–84)

Decision criteria

The decision to employ recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) must be anchored first on the engineered performance of the pavement—its strength, durability and safety—so that functional requirements are never compromised. After confirming the required material properties through appropriate mixture design or construction adjustments, cost considerations and the magnitude and duration of environmental and public impacts become the next controlling factors. The decision to employ recycled concrete aggregate should be guided chiefly by cost, schedule, and sustainability considerations; using RCA lowers material expenses, speeds construction through on‑site recycling, and reduces the volume of concrete requiring disposal, making these three criteria the primary drivers. The decision to employ recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) should be governed primarily by its sustainability advantage—its capacity for carbon sequestration and overall reduction of the pavement’s carbon footprint—provided that the material can satisfy performance and durability requirements comparable to virgin aggregate. Economic considerations are the next controlling factor; the lower capital outlay, extended service life, and reduced maintenance costs make RCA financially attractive. Finally, safety and public‑impact criteria must be addressed through stakeholder engagement and demonstrable benefits such as improved skid resistance, reduced disruption, noise reduction and overall societal acceptance. [1][2][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [2] — Benefits (p. 70); [4] — Ch. 6 (pp. 78–84)

The guides first advise clarifying sustainability priorities and then evaluating each alternative against a defined factor set. In general, these considerations involve the following: priorities and values of the organization or project, pavement performance, cost, impact magnitude and duration, and risk. The hierarchy begins with preserving engineered pavement performance; any sustainability gains must not degrade strength, durability, or serviceability. It is critical that, while striving to improve sustainability by using RCA, the engineering performance of the final pavement should not be compromised. Recycled concrete aggregate should be considered an engineered material for which the properties must be determined prior to use so that appropriate mixture design or construction adjustments can be made as required. Once performance is assured, economic, environmental and societal benefits are evaluated. Economically the benefits include reduced capital investment and greatly increased service life with lower maintenance and rehabilitation costs. Environmental benefits include a smaller carbon footprint, reduced expended energy, and less overall pollution and waste. Societal aspects include reduced disruption, noise reduction, and improved safety through increased skid resistance. The source indicates that when evaluating recycled concrete aggregate utilization, the economic, environmental, and societal criteria should all be regarded as essential components of a sustainable pavement solution; decisions must balance cost savings, carbon‑footprint and waste reductions, and social impacts such as reduced disruption and improved safety rather than privileging one over the others. Ultimately, the consideration of trade‑offs is essentially a benefit/cost analysis done more holistically (i.e., considering more than just economics). A consistent, systematic approach to this holistic analysis helps avoid unintended bias and aligns the selected option with the defined values and constraints. [1][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [4] — Ch. 6 (p. 84)

Analysis methods and tools

To decide whether to incorporate recycled concrete aggregate, the methodology relies on a holistic benefit‑cost analysis that evaluates multiple sustainability metrics rather than only initial construction cost. The analysis compares alternatives—such as locally sourced virgin aggregate versus distant RCA—and quantifies life‑cycle cost, greenhouse‑gas emissions, energy use and other impacts while explicitly considering risk, performance expectations, and the project’s priorities and values. By applying consistent criteria—including pavement performance, cost, impact magnitude and duration—the approach provides a structured framework for weighing trade‑offs and selecting the option that best meets the project’s sustainability objectives. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 28)

The analyses rest on a set of explicit assumptions that shape projected sustainability benefits and on several sources of uncertainty that can alter performance or economic outcomes. A common logistical premise is that “the nearest source of RCA is 100 mi away while an acceptable local extracted aggregate is only 5 mi away,” which frames opportunity‑cost calculations for transport emissions and material costs. Chemical and mix design assumptions include the expectation that “the inclusion of the RCA would require mixture adjustments that require an additional 50 lb of portland cement per cubic yard of concrete” and that the free calcium hydroxide in the recycled aggregate will carbonate in situ, a process whose effectiveness is described as varying with environmental conditions: “The rate of sequestration varies directly with temperature, surface area, relative humidity, and concentration of CO.” The guides also assume that RCA can function across pavement layers, allowing substitution for higher‑grade natural aggregates.

Uncertainties arise from material variability and agency practice. Because “Recycled concrete aggregate should be considered an engineered material for which the properties must be determined prior to use so that appropriate mixture design or construction adjustments can be made as required,” differences in source concrete, processing methods, and local testing regimes introduce risk of compromised performance. Additionally, the sensitivity of carbonation to temperature, humidity, CO₂ levels, and actual exposed surface area creates uncertainty around long‑term carbon sequestration benefits. Variations in RCA quality also raise questions about fatigue resistance and service life, while stakeholder perception—whether recycled concrete is viewed as a valuable resource or waste—affects acceptance and implementation. [1][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [4] — Ch. 6 (pp. 78–84)

Stakeholders and constraints

The decision to use recycled concrete aggregate must involve the key stakeholders identified in the guidance: the transportation agency that will implement the project, the overseeing environmental agency, as well as contractors and RCA producers, while the public’s perception also needs to be considered. These parties provide technical input and address concerns, but final approval typically rests with the responsible transportation authority, often after coordination with the environmental regulator. [4]

[4] — Ch. 6 (p. 78)

Across the guides, the use of recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) is bounded primarily by design and performance standards rather than distinct permitting or funding mandates. Projects must adjust mix designs to accommodate RCA’s lower strength, often requiring supplemental cement, and must verify that the engineered material meets all required properties before placement. The final pavement must satisfy FHWA‑issued performance criteria so that sustainability gains do not compromise structural integrity. In addition, compliance with AASHTO specifications governing both unbound base courses and coarse aggregate in hydraulic cement concrete is mandatory, and agency clearance—including transportation and environmental approvals—is required before RCA can be incorporated. [1][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 28–32); [4] — Ch. 6 (p. 78)

Timing and implementation

Across the guides, the decision to employ recycled concrete aggregate is recommended during project planning and design—before finalizing mixture designs—so that its engineered properties can be evaluated early and any required mix‑design or construction adjustments incorporated, while also allowing environmental mitigation strategies, legislative considerations, and alternative options to be addressed prior to construction. [1][3]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 32); [3] — Ch. 7 (p. 69)

When existing pavement shows signs of alkali‑silica reaction or D‑cracking, that condition triggers the decision to recycle the concrete into RCA for reuse in any pavement layer; likewise, projects aiming to lower material costs or speed construction may activate on‑site recycling plants to supply RCA promptly. These events prompt stakeholders to select RCA over disposal, supporting cost and schedule goals while reducing waste. [2]

Outcomes and follow-up

Success for recycled concrete aggregate utilization is achieved when RCA is treated as a valued, non‑waste source of aggregate that can replace virgin material on a ton‑for‑ton basis and is incorporated into pavement designs—typically in the base, subbase, or lower lift of a composite or two‑lift concrete pavement—in a way that delivers net sustainability advantages. Success is evident when all key stakeholders (transportation agencies, regulators, contractors, producers, and the public) recognize this value, when the engineered mix satisfies required structural and surface performance, and when measurable economic, environmental, and societal benefits are realized. These outcomes are measured through a holistic benefit‑cost analysis that applies consistent metrics; “Some examples of sustainability value include life-cycle cost, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, energy use, water/air quality, waste generation, scenic views quality, art, community context, history, habitat continuity, and performance life.” The proportion of RCA used is tracked, cost savings are documented, and reductions in material‑transport impacts are verified—“In combination, this strategy not only lowers costs, but also reduces environmental impacts, primarily by reducing the environmental burden of transporting materials.” Stakeholder acceptance is recorded, reflecting that “Success requires a common understanding that recycled concrete is not a waste product and that concrete has added value after it has been removed and processed.” Economic performance is quantified by comparing capital outlay and service‑life extensions, as “Economically the benefits include reduced capital investment and greatly increased service life with lower maintenance and rehabilitation costs.” Environmental improvements are captured through reductions in carbon footprint, energy consumption, and waste, while societal indicators such as construction disruption, noise levels, and skid‑resistance performance are monitored to confirm that the pavement meets sustainable design goals. [1][4]

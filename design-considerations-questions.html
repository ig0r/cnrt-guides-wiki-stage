1. Purpose and decision context

What decision is Design Considerations intended to support?

Design Considerations help the engineer decide which structural and functional performance targets, service‑life expectations, and concrete material specifications should be adopted for a given concrete pavement project. By defining the required level of structural and functional performance, the target design life, and the acceptable ranges for strength, stiffness, dimensional stability and other properties, the designer can select the appropriate configuration—slab thickness, reinforcement, load‑transfer details, subgrade support, and concrete strength and elastic modulus—to meet those objectives while resisting environmental effects. The concept therefore guides the decision of which concrete material characteristics (strength, stiffness, dimensional stability, durability) to use as inputs that determine slab thickness and overall structural capacity, ensuring the pavement will achieve its intended performance over the specified service life. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–56)

What problem, need, or performance goal does Design Considerations address?

Design Considerations address the fundamental problem of ensuring that a concrete pavement can meet both its structural capacity and functional performance requirements throughout its intended service life. The goal is to define target levels for key material attributes—strength, stiffness, dimensional stability, and durability—so that the pavement can safely support present and future heavy traffic loads while providing a safe, comfortable ride and resisting environmental stresses such as thermal movement and drying shrinkage. By specifying these property thresholds and using performance‑based specifications, designers aim to prevent load‑related cracking, achieve required early‑age strength for construction traffic, and guarantee long‑term reliability. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–56), Ch. 6 (pp. 161–162), Ch. 10 (p. 325)

2. Scope and applicability

For what project types, asset conditions, or stages of project development is Design Considerations appropriate?

Design considerations apply to any concrete‑pavement undertaking, whether a new installation or work on an existing asset, because every project requires the designer to establish the intended structural and functional performance, the target service life, and the required levels of concrete properties such as strength, stiffness and dimensional stability. These decisions are made during the design stage of the project, before construction or rehabilitation begins, ensuring that the pavement will meet the defined performance objectives throughout its intended lifespan. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–53)

When is Design Considerations not appropriate, or when is a different approach preferable?

When a pavement design depends only on compressive strength as a stand‑in for other performance traits—particularly when early high strength is achieved with chemical admixtures or heating that can create a poorer microstructure and higher permeability—the approach is not appropriate because durability may be compromised. In those cases the designer should switch to a method that directly tests the required properties such as flexural strength, fatigue resistance, or abrasion resistance according to the relevant standards rather than inferring them from strength alone. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 6 (pp. 161–162)

3. Information needs

What condition data, site information, cost data, traffic data, environmental information, or stakeholder input does Design Considerations need?

Design Considerations must be supplied with a defined level of structural performance—reflecting imposed truck loads—and the functional performance required for safety and ride quality, together with a target service life or design life. The designer must also specify concrete property levels, including strength, stiffness (elastic modulus), dimensional stability (thermal movement and drying shrinkage) and permeability, as well as fundamental engineering properties such as resilient modulus and fatigue characteristics. Site‑specific condition data are required, notably subgrade support conditions and overall structural conditions of the existing pavement. Traffic information must capture expected heavy traffic loadings, while environmental data should address influences that affect performance (e.g., temperature cycles, moisture). Predictors representing roadbed attributes complete the input set for performance‑based models. Cost figures and stakeholder preferences are not explicitly called out in these specifications. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–54), Ch. 10 (p. 325)

4. Alternatives

What feasible options should be compared under Design Considerations?

The designer must compare a defined set of feasible options that govern both structural capacity and functional performance. These alternatives include slab thickness; reinforcement and load‑transfer configurations; concrete strength levels (compressive strength); elastic modulus or mix stiffness; dimensional stability expressed through drying shrinkage and thermal‑expansion behavior; permeability; and the suite of durability‑related characteristics. As the report states, the designer must “compare acceptable levels of key concrete properties such as strength, stiffness, dimensional stability and permeability” and also evaluate “slab thickness, reinforcement and load‑transfer configurations, concrete strength levels, elastic modulus (stiffness) of the mix, and the quality of subgrade support conditions.” Moreover, design considerations require comparing concrete mixes based on “variations in compressive strength, mix stiffness, the degree of dimensional stability expressed through drying shrinkage and thermal‑expansion behavior, and the suite of durability‑related characteristics” to meet the targeted service life. [1]

5. Decision criteria

Which criteria should control the decision for Design Considerations: safety, performance, cost, durability, schedule, sustainability, accessibility, or public impact?

The decision for design considerations in concrete pavements should be governed primarily by performance criteria. Performance includes the pavement’s structural capacity to carry imposed truck traffic loads and its functional ability to provide safety and a comfortable, quiet ride, while durability is treated as an essential supporting requirement that must be ensured through material testing. Cost, schedule, sustainability, accessibility, and public impact are not identified as controlling factors in the source. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–56), Ch. 10 (p. 325)

How should competing criteria for Design Considerations be weighted or prioritized?

When balancing the various design considerations for a concrete pavement, the designer should first give priority to structural capacity, using concrete strength as the dominant input for slab thickness because it most directly governs the ability to carry traffic loads. Next, functional performance and the desired service life are set, after which stiffness and dimensional stability are evaluated to fine‑tune ride quality and crack control, recognizing that they have historically been secondary to strength. Finally, durability is addressed through material testing and specifications rather than as a direct design variable, ensuring long‑term performance without weighting it as a primary structural factor. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–53)

6. Analysis methods and tools

What assumptions and uncertainties influence the results for Design Considerations?

The design outcome assumes that the engineer first establishes a target service life, defines the desired structural and functional performance levels, and specifies acceptable ranges for concrete strength, stiffness, dimensional stability and permeability. It further assumes that these concrete properties are known and can be entered as inputs to the design model, while other traits are either assumed when calculating slab thickness or omitted entirely. In practice, measured compressive strength is often used as a proxy for broader suitability, including abrasion resistance and durability. This reliance on assumed values introduces uncertainty because durability cannot be measured directly, stiffness and thermal movement/shrinkage have traditionally been excluded from calculations, and the correlation between strength and other performance characteristics is not reliable. Early‑age high strength achieved through admixtures or heating may degrade the microstructure, increase permeability, and reduce durability, leading to potential discrepancies between predicted and actual field behavior. [1]

7. Stakeholders and constraints

Who needs to provide input, make the decision, or approve it for Design Considerations?

The source indicates that the responsibility for supplying the input and making decisions on design considerations rests with the pavement designer, who must define the required structural and functional performance levels, target service life, and the specific concrete property thresholds needed to achieve optimal performance. Contractors provide input about the need for early‑strength concrete so that construction traffic can be allowed onto the pavement, while the owner determines and approves the ultimate strength requirements that are needed at later ages, thereby shaping the design considerations. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–53), Ch. 6 (pp. 161–162)

What permits, standards, policies, funding rules, or design requirements constrain the choice for Design Considerations?

The selection of concrete‑pavement design considerations is limited by the project‑specific requirements that must be defined: a prescribed level of structural and functional performance, a target service or design life, and minimum thresholds for concrete properties such as strength, stiffness, dimensional stability and permeability. These criteria are embedded in the pavement design specifications and material‑construction standards, which set the property limits needed to achieve the desired performance over the intended lifespan. [1]

IMCP — Ch. 3 (pp. 52–53)

8. Timing and implementation

What events, thresholds, or deadlines would trigger action for Design Considerations?

Action is triggered when the concrete reaches the strength level required for its intended purpose, whether that is an early‑age target to allow construction traffic or a later‑age target specified by the owner, establishing a deadline based on the needed age. If the observed rate of strength development indicates an increased risk of early‑age cracking, corrective actions such as mix adjustments or altered curing must be taken. These strength‑and‑age thresholds dictate when design decisions are implemented to ensure performance and durability. [1]

9. Outcomes and follow-up

What does success look like for Design Considerations, and how will it be measured?

“Success for design considerations is realized when the designer establishes and then meets defined structural and functional performance levels—ensuring the pavement can carry imposed truck loads while providing safety and a comfortable, quiet ride—for the targeted service life, and simultaneously achieves the required concrete property thresholds such as strength, stiffness, dimensional stability and permeability.” “Success for design considerations is achieved when a concrete pavement exhibits the required structural performance—its ability to support current and future heavy traffic while withstanding environmental influences—and does so without showing load‑related cracking such as corner, transverse, longitudinal or shattered slabs.” “Success for design considerations is achieved when the concrete pavement meets the specified target values for fundamental engineering properties—most notably resilient modulus and fatigue resistance—that are known predictors of long‑term performance.” These success criteria are measured by (1) confirming structural capacity through load‑capacity analysis and verifying functional attributes with ride‑quality evaluation; (2) validating material test results—compressive strength, permeability, elastic modulus, resilient modulus, fatigue resistance—against the specifications for concrete properties; (3) checking that principal design variables (slab thickness, reinforcement, load transfer mechanisms, subgrade support) meet their intended levels; and (4) monitoring the pavement over time for the absence of load‑related cracking. The measured property values are then entered into primary prediction models to forecast stress, distress, or overall performance under anticipated traffic, environmental, roadbed, and structural conditions.

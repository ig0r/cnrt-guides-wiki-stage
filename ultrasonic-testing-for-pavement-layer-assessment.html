Ultrasonic Testing for Pavement Layer Assessment

non-destructive-testingpavement-structureground-penetrating-radarultrasonic-testingvoid-detection
Created 2026-09-09 · Updated 2026-09-09

Ultrasonic testing, often paired with ground‑penetrating radar, is a nondestructive technique that measures reflected acoustic energy to locate subsurface voids beneath pavement panels showing early‑stage longitudinal top‑down or low‑severity corner cracks before the slab has fully broken or settled [1]. By detecting acoustic contrasts it can quantify the size and extent of these hidden cavities, provide exact layer‑thickness values and identify embedded reinforcement, thereby informing whether remedial action is required and supporting short‑term repair planning as well as longer‑term design decisions [1][2]. Because it relies on acoustic rather than dielectric properties, the method is applicable to any pavement type or layer configuration that exhibits such material‑related cracking [1].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The ultrasonic testing investigation evaluates subsurface voids beneath pavement panels that exhibit early‑stage cracking—whether longitudinal top‑down cracks or low‑severity corner cracks—that have not yet fully broken or settled. It seeks to determine if a void exists under the crack, quantify its size or extent, understand how the void may influence crack propagation, and decide whether remedial action is required before full panel failure. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 137–175)

Ultrasonic echo imaging, often used together with GPR, can reveal voids beneath longitudinal top‑down cracks before the pavement panel has fully broken or settled. By identifying these hidden cavities early, engineers can prioritize inspection and plan targeted maintenance or rehabilitation actions before more extensive damage occurs, thereby informing both short‑term repair schedules and longer‑term design adjustments. In addition, ultrasonic testing delivers quantitative data on the pavement’s structural condition, exact layer‑thickness values, and the location of embedded reinforcement or subsurface voids; this information lets engineers evaluate whether a given section meets design criteria, prioritize maintenance actions, and select appropriate preservation or rehabilitation treatments, thereby increasing confidence in planning and decision making. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 137); [2] — Ch. 3 (p. 50)

Scope and applicability

“Ultrasonic testing (often paired with GPR) is appropriate for locating voids beneath pavement panels that are showing early‑stage longitudinal top‑down cracking, before the slab has completely fractured or settled.” It is also suitable when “Ultrasonic testing is appropriate for detecting voids that lie beneath low‑severity corner cracks which have not yet fully broken or settled.” Because the technique relies on acoustic contrasts rather than specific structural composition, it can be applied to any pavement exhibiting these material‑related cracks, regardless of pavement type or layer configuration. [1]

Visual inspection and condition assessment

Ultrasonic testing should focus on locations where longitudinal top‑down cracking is evident, because such cracks often signal the development of underlying voids before the pavement panel fully breaks and settles. It should also be directed at visible low‑severity corner cracks, using the technique to locate any underlying voids that have not yet progressed to full breakage or settlement. [1]

Field measurements and testing

Field assessments of pavement layers employ the primary nondestructive methods Ground‑Penetrating Radar (GPR) and ultrasonic echo imaging. GPR identifies dielectric contrasts that reveal empty spaces, enabling detection of subsurface voids beneath longitudinal top‑down cracks or low‑severity corner cracks and providing an estimate of their size or extent. Ultrasonic testing measures reflected acoustic energy to pinpoint void locations, offering an indication of whether a void is present and early warning of delamination or loss of support. [1]

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Ultrasonic testing, together with GPR, can support the conclusion that these methods are useful for detecting voids beneath longitudinal top‑down cracks before the pavement panel fully separates, indicating early‑stage distress detection capability. The source states that “GPR and ultrasonic testing devices may be useful in detecting the presence of voids” where “longitudinal top‑down cracking has initiated but the panel has not yet fully broken and settled.” In addition, the techniques can confirm whether voids exist under low‑severity corner cracks that are still open and can estimate the void’s size or extent; as noted, “GPR and ultrasonic testing devices may be useful in identifying the presence and size or extent of voids beneath low‑severity corner cracks that have not yet fully broken and settled.” However, this capability is confined to such low‑severity corner cracks, and the method’s reliability for other crack types, deeper defects, or more advanced damage remains uncertain. The source does not provide further information on remaining uncertainties or limitations. [1]

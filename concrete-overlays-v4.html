Concrete Overlays

Use this page for: deciding whether a concrete overlay is viable, selecting the overlay system, designing and specifying it, releasing the work to paving, and accepting it. It is a cross-guide synthesis, not a substitute for a project-specific structural design, a pavement evaluation, or the governing agency specification.

Evidence boundary. The Guide to Concrete Overlays, 4th ed. [1] is the primary technical voice; the specialized overlay guides fill individual facets, [2] supplies the preservation positioning, and PennDOT Publication 242, Ch. 12 [3] supplies the Pennsylvania eligibility layer (cited by section number). Numeric values carry the guide page they come from. Where the guides disagree, both readings are given in place rather than silently reconciled.

Overview

Definition and purpose

A concrete overlay is a layer of portland cement concrete (PCC) placed over an existing asphalt, composite, or concrete pavement [1, p. 2]. Overlays are grouped into four types by substrate and interface — concrete-on-asphalt bonded/unbonded (COA–B, COA–U) and concrete-on-concrete bonded/unbonded (COC–B, COC–U) [1, p. 5]. In a bonded overlay the two layers act as one monolithic slab with a single neutral axis, lowering flexural stress and deflection; in an unbonded overlay the layers bend independently, each with its own neutral axis [1, p. 16], [1, p. 81]. The purpose is to preserve or rehabilitate while retaining the equity in the existing pavement [1, p. 36].

The system decision is not a surface-treatment choice. A bonded overlay depends on composite action and therefore on a sound, prepared interface; an unbonded overlay uses the existing pavement as a foundation and therefore depends on uniform support, drainage, and a compatible separation strategy where the underlying pavement is concrete [1], [4].

Between 2000 and 2017, 71% of US concrete overlays were on asphalt and 29% on concrete [1, p. 5]; at least 46 states built ~1,289 overlays through 2017 [1, p. 3]. Across more than 1,000 projects, roughly 75% are unbonded and 25% bonded [4, p. 2].

The controlling structural concern is uniformity of support, not a high support value. An overlay can be designed for a range of support strengths, but abrupt changes in support cause distress. Drainage, rocking slabs, broken concrete, joints, localized weak areas, and edge conditions are therefore selection inputs, not construction afterthoughts [1], [7].

Classification is contested — thin "preservation" vs. thick "rehabilitation"

[1] itself splits overlays by thickness: thin COC overlays (2–4 in.) are a preservation strategy and ≥6 in. overlays a rehabilitation strategy [1, p. 36]. [2] (Ch. 11) instead places overlays — including thicker unbonded ones that "add structural capacity" — in the preservation toolbox and argues against that ≤4 in. cutoff [2, p. 242], even while quoting the FHWA definition that preservation "do[es] not add capacity or structural value" [2, p. 8]. PennDOT Pub. 242 sides with the thickness/structural split (see the Pennsylvania callouts below). So whether a given overlay "is preservation" depends on the governing jurisdiction, not on the treatment.

Application

When to use — candidates by type

Type selection follows existing condition, traffic, geometry, and desired life [1, p. 4]; the existing pavement is rated good/fair/poor/deteriorated by a six-step evaluation [1, p. 8]. Every project must be cored (2–4 cores/lane-mile by functional class) to verify thickness and condition and to check asphalt stripping, with FWD for support and GPR for layer thickness [1, p. 13]. A feasibility study combines records review, distress mapping, field inspection, and targeted coring or testing; it must not select an overlay family from a single visual rating [2], [8].

  • COA–U — asphalt/composite with significant HMA deterioration (rutting, potholes, alligator cracking, shoving, pumping) [1, p. 27]; needs ≥3–4 in. of sound asphalt as a construction platform [1, p. 28].
  • COA–B — asphalt/composite in fair-or-better structural condition; ≥3 in. sound asphalt for bond [1, p. 30], [1, p. 82].
  • COC–U — existing concrete in poor-to-deteriorated condition (even with slow-acting MRD) provided support is stable and uniform; ≥4 in. concrete platform [1, p. 36], [1, p. 38].
  • COC–B — existing concrete in good-to-excellent condition with remaining life ≥ the overlay's; a narrowly applicable, infrequent case [1, p. 20], [1, p. 114], [11, p. 3].
  • Thin (≤5 in.) overlays are a preservation technique for concrete pavements with deteriorated joints; the key is milling off the joint deterioration before overlaying [5, p. 1].
  • Parking lots / low-volume — COA–B for low-to-medium-severity distress, COA–U for high-severity over stable support [14, p. 1], [14, p. 11].

Concrete overlays are especially appropriate where reconstruction would discard a usable foundation, where durability and lower-maintenance service are wanted, or where staged construction has value. The project must also be able to absorb the added elevation and the resulting drainage, tie-in, utility, curb, and traffic-control changes [1], [14].

When not to use one yet

Disqualifying conditions
  • No bonded overlay where the substrate has significant structural deterioration, inadequate base/subbase, or asphalt stripping/delamination — use unbonded instead [1, p. 25], [1, p. 30].
  • No concrete overlay over fast-acting MRD (fast D-cracking or ASR) [5, p. 2].
  • No COC–B where joints show MRD, poor support/drainage, vertical-clearance limits, or numerous random cracks hard to reproduce in the overlay [1, p. 115], [6, p. 2].
  • Never use concrete shoulders with a COA–B (edge support promotes bond loss) [1, p. 96].
  • If, after milling, extensive partial- or full-depth repairs would still be required, the pavement is not a good bonded candidate (it may still suit unbonded) [5, p. 2].

Four project-level gates must also be closed before the overlay decision is made at all:

  1. Support. Existing support is nonuniform or unstable — rocking slabs, severe breakup, widespread loss of support, abrupt structural transitions. Investigate and repair the cause first [1], [7].
  2. Drainage. Water cannot be carried away from the pavement system. An overlay alone does not correct a drainage defect [1], [2].
  3. Geometry. Required grade changes create unresolved conflicts at bridges, curbs, gutters, inlets, utilities, sidewalks, or ADA routes. Parking-lot work commonly exposes these fixed-elevation constraints [1], [14].
  4. Scope. The condition is so widespread that full-depth reconstruction, full-depth reclamation with cement, or another strategy is more appropriate [15], [16].

Decision path: select an overlay system

  1. Establish the candidate condition. Review history, layer data, traffic, drainage, distresses, and field evidence; core and test where the condition or layer structure is uncertain — coring is mandatory, not optional [1, p. 13], [2], [8].
  2. Resolve foundation and drainage defects. If support will remain nonuniform after practical repairs, pause the overlay decision and evaluate reconstruction or foundation rehabilitation [1], [7].
  3. Classify the existing pavement. Existing asphalt or composite leads to a COA decision; existing concrete leads to a COC decision [1].
  4. Decide whether composite bond is dependable. For COA–B the asphalt must be good, or economically restorable to good, with ≥3 in. sound material [1, p. 30]. For COC–B the existing concrete must be good to excellent and the project must control surface preparation, bonding, and joint compatibility [1, p. 114]. If those conditions are not met, evaluate the unbonded counterpart [4].
  5. For COC–U, choose a separation-layer strategy. Asphalt and nonwoven geotextile are the two common choices. Evaluate drainage, existing-pavement condition, traffic, profile, material behavior, and constructability; do not specify fabric by generic weight alone [1, p. 50], [17, p. 3], [4].
  6. Complete structural design and constructability review. Determine thickness, jointing, load transfer, fiber use, shoulder and edge details, staging, and opening criteria using the governing agency method. This page does not replace the overlay design procedure [6], [1].
Pennsylvania — what counts as preservation (PennDOT Pub. 242, Ch. 12)

On a PennDOT project eligibility is governed by Pub. 242, and it diverges from [1]/[2] exactly on structural overlays:

  • A preservation project shall not "increase the structural depth of the existing pavement to increase pavement life"; a first-time concrete-pavement overlay must meet the minimum depths of Tables 9.5/10.4 ([3, §12.2.C.2]). Appendix A, Table 1 classes a "structural overlay" as rehabilitation.
  • PennDOT's eligible rigid-preservation list contains no concrete-on-concrete overlay; the only concrete overlay it admits as preservation is an ultra-thin bonded concrete overlay of asphalt, as a non-structural wearing course ([3, §12.2.A.1.o]).
  • So an unbonded (or thick bonded) overlay is not preservation in PA — it is rehabilitation/3R, requiring a full pavement design and Pavement Type Selection, not the "no design required" preservation path ([3, §12.3]). Document the structural-vs-non-structural rationale in the Pavement Type Selection Memo ([3, §12.5]), since a reviewer reading [1]/[2] may expect overlays to qualify.

Implementation

Materials

  • Cementitious — Type I/II (ASTM C150), blended (C595), or hydraulic (C1157); Type III not recommended (shrinkage cracking), so early-strength cement is not a universal overlay solution [1, p. 44]. SCM replacement typically 15–35%, and 20–40% at 65–75 °F mixture temperature [1, p. 44], [1, p. 46].
  • Aggregate (ASTM C33) — maximum coarse-aggregate size ≤ ⅓ of the overlay thickness [1, p. 44]. w/cm typically 0.40–0.45; paste volume ≤25% for shrinkage control [1, p. 45], [1, p. 46].
  • Macrofibers — synthetic 3–8 lb/yd³ or steel 25–75 lb/yd³ (≈0.2–0.5% by volume); they improve post-cracking toughness, giving effective flexural strength f_eff = MOR + f_150 (ASTM C1609, f_150 typically 100–200 psi). Fibers do not replace dowels for faulting control, and microfibers give no structural capacity [1, p. 47], [1, p. 49]. Fiber reinforcement is recommended particularly for overlays ≤4 in. [5, p. 13]. Use them only where the structural design specifies residual strength and a trial batch has confirmed placement and finishing [18].
  • Tie and dowel bars — dowels (ASTM A1078) are not used below ~7 in.; tie bars Grade 60 No. 4 typical at 30 in. spacing, not recommended below a 5 in. overlay [1, p. 51], [10].
  • Full-depth-repair concrete designed for 3,000 psi at 28 days; deteriorated-joint fill uses 2,000 psi flowable mortar [1, p. 69], [1, p. 40].
  • COC–B only — overlay aggregate CTE and modulus should match or be lower than the existing pavement to limit debonding stress (asphalt E ≈ 400,000 psi vs. concrete ≈ 4,000,000 psi) [1, p. 44], [1, p. 82].
  • Curing compound and joint material — white-pigmented membrane curing compound (ASTM C309, ASTM C1315, or AASHTO M 148) on the surface and exposed vertical faces; joint-material selection follows climate and the drainage behavior of the joint system [1, p. 74], [19].
  • Recycled and supplementary materials — recycled concrete aggregate, ternary mixtures, and sustainability-driven substitutions are evaluated against the mix design, exposure, and performance requirements, not assumed beneficial; they do not relax overlay-specific design and QA requirements [20], [21], [22].

Separation layer / interlayer (unbonded overlays)

An unbonded concrete-on-concrete overlay requires a separation layer; over asphalt (COA–U) it usually needs none [6, p. 3], [1, p. 19]. The layer must balance three functions: isolation (a shear plane that relieves stress and stops reflective cracking), drainage, and bedding (a cushion reducing curling/warping and keying) [17, p. 3]. Two materials dominate — a nominal 1–2 in. asphalt layer or a nonwoven geotextile [1, p. 50], [5, p. 14]. Asphalt layers can strip and cause loss of support; geotextile eliminates that risk and speeds construction [17, p. 3].

Cross-guide discrepancy — geotextile mass by thickness

The authoritative interlayer source ([17], corroborated by [4] and [5]) specifies 13.3 oz/yd² (<5 in.) and 14.7 oz/yd² (≥5 in.) [17, p. 4], [4, p. 37], [5, p. 15]. [1] rounds these to 13.0 oz/yd² @130 mils and 15.0 oz/yd² @170 mils [1, p. 50]. Use the 13.3 / 14.7 specification; the difference is a rounding in the 4th-ed. guide, not a real design change. Field practice trends thinner under thin overlays (MnDOT is evaluating 8 oz/yd² under a 3 in. overlay) [17, p. 6].

Equipment

Plan conventional concrete-paving equipment plus what the selected system demands, and confirm the plan in a pre-paving meeting with inspection responsibility assigned in the project quality plan [12], [9].

  • Milling / removal — carbide cold-planing machines; depth by stringline, ski, or 3D control [1, p. 69], [1, p. 76]. Heavy overlay removal wants ≥800–1,000 hp machines with a coarse-cutting drum and concrete-specific carbide teeth [11, p. 31].
  • Pavers — conventional slipform, stringless (3D), and zero-clearance/zero-offset pavers, some with dowel-bar inserters; stringless needs only 2–3 ft lateral clearance vs. 6–10 ft for stringline [1, p. 60], [1, p. 72].
  • Saws — early-entry lightweight saws let crews on sooner; extra saws are needed for short joint spacing [1, p. 33], [1, p. 74].
  • Cleaning / surface prep — sweeper or air blower; ~150 psi compressed air to clean deteriorated joints; COC–B requires shotblasting (most effective) to remove milling microcracking [1, p. 70], [1, p. 118].
  • Geotextile securing — adhesive spray, or pins/nails through 2.0–2.75 in. galvanized discs at ≤6 ft spacing [1, p. 71], [17, p. 7].

Procedure (construction sequence)

  1. Pre-overlay repairs — limited for unbonded (only distresses that would reflect through), more extensive for COA–B to restore fair/good condition; rocking slabs get full-depth repair. Localized repairs must not create new abrupt support changes [1, p. 68], [1, p. 69], [23], [7].
  2. Milling (asphalt surfaces) — optional; remove non-structural distress and rutting, expose sound asphalt, add bond texture. If the base is exposed, mill 2–3 in. extra and thicken the overlay by the same amount — and tell the sawing crew [1, p. 68].
  3. Deteriorated-joint repair (COC–U) — clean with hand tools and ~150 psi air, fill flush with flowable mortar before the separation layer [1, p. 70].
  4. Separation layer (COC–U) — asphalt (control in-place air voids) or geotextile: mill faulting >0.25 in., unroll taut and wrinkle-free, overlap 6–10 in. with ≤3 layers at any point, daylight to the edge or underdrain, secure by adhesive or pins ≤6 ft o.c.; place fabric ≤2–3 days before paving [1, p. 70], [1, p. 71], [17, p. 7].
  5. Geometry and load-transfer set-up — verify grade control, joint layout, basket location, anchorage and alignment, tie bars, and the planned saw-cut sequence before the first load of concrete; use a formal basket inspection where dowels are specified [10], [9], [24].
  6. Placement — sweep; keep the surface damp with no free water; wet and cool if ≥120 °F; bid concrete by volume (yd³) and placement by area (yd²) [1, p. 72], [1, p. 29].
  7. Curing — white-pigmented curing compound on surface and vertical faces immediately after texturing; ~150 ft²/gal in two coats for overlays <7 in. [1, p. 74]. Select texture for the project's friction and noise goals and confirm it suits the overlay system [25], [26].
  8. Sawing — timely, T/3, through the slab edge; extra saws for short spacing [1, p. 75].
  9. Sealing / filling — per type and climate; low-speed (≤45 mph) roads are designed with filled joints flush to the surface [1, p. 75], [11, p. 27].
  10. Open to traffic — per opening-strength criteria (maturity method), after early-age protection is complete [1, p. 41], [14, p. 3], [27].

COC–B swaps in shotblast surface preparation and matched joint sawing (over each existing joint), with no grout coat [1, p. 117], [1, p. 118].

Environmental and operational limits

Hard limits and their sources
  • When the asphalt or separation-layer surface is ≥120 °F (49 °C), wet and cool it and keep it free of standing water to reduce early-age cracking [1, p. 29], [5, p. 22].
  • COC–B must not be placed when the mixture and existing-pavement temperatures differ by more than 35 °F; hot, dry, windy, low-humidity conditions are worst (evaporation → debonding) [1, p. 119].
  • Thin unbonded overlays have a shorter sawing window (higher surface-area-to-volume ratio); keep early wheel loads 1–2 ft off free edges [1, p. 37], [1, p. 75].
  • Cold weather: SCMs slow set — use blankets, heated mixtures, or SCM-free mixes [1, p. 47].
  • Everything else — wind, precipitation, delivery time, opening strength — is set project-specifically in the contract documents and quality plan. The guides give practices and acceptance controls, not universal numeric weather limits [12], [9], [28].

Release-to-placement checklist

A field hold-point list. Agency specifications and the approved quality plan remain controlling.

  • Existing-pavement evaluation, core and FWD results, design basis, repair limits, and drainage details are available to the field team [1, p. 13], [2].
  • Required milling, removals, partial-/full-depth repairs, and localized foundation repairs are accepted; no rocking slabs, open voids, or unaddressed unstable areas remain [11], [23], [7].
  • Pre-overlay faulting is within limit for the chosen interlayer — ≤⅜ in. under 1 in. asphalt, ≤¼ in. under geotextile [1, p. 38].
  • The bonded surface is shotblast-prepared and clean (COC–B), or the unbonded separation layer is the approved type, continuous, overlapped 6–10 in. with ≤3 layers, daylighted, secured, and free of standing water [1, p. 71], [17, p. 7], [4].
  • Grade, cross slope, transition tapers, tie-ins, drainage inlets, utilities, and fixed-elevation constraints are checked [1, p. 61], [14].
  • Dowel baskets, tie bars, and joint layout are inspected for location, elevation, alignment, anchorage, and damage; deviations are dispositioned before paving [10], [9], [24].
  • The approved mixture, trial-batch results where fibers are used, material certificates, curing compound, and test equipment are ready [1, p. 44], [18], [27].
  • Surface temperature is below 120 °F, or cooling is in place, and the sawing plan matches the panel layout and expected window [1, p. 29], [1, p. 75].
  • Paving, curing, saw-cutting, traffic control, inspection staffing, and nonconformance response are coordinated in the pre-paving plan [12], [9], [28].

Quality control and acceptance

Typical acceptance items are thickness, strength, air content, combined gradation, and smoothness [1, p. 5]. The cured surface and vertical faces must appear uniformly white "like a sheet of paper," free of streaks [1, p. 74].

Pennsylvania — verification and submission (PennDOT Pub. 242, Ch. 12)

PennDOT's pre-work verification also gates eligibility: FWD to confirm failed joints and <10% patching ([3, §12.3.B]), coring for milling depth ([3, §12.3.A.2]), IRI + friction data, a Scoping Field View ([3, §12.4]), and a Pavement Type Selection Memo to HDTS ([3, §12.5]). These are the auditable form of [1]'s mandatory-coring and uniform-support requirement.

Performance and service life

Overlays commonly deliver 20-year low-maintenance service lives, and many reach 30–40 years [1, p. 2]. By type: COA–U has 30+ years of good-to-excellent performance in many states [1, p. 27]; COC–U has been used 40+ years with life comparable to a new full-depth pavement [1, p. 36]; COC–B extends life 20–25 years (some >35), with a 15-year minimum before maintenance [1, p. 114]. [11] frames three performance stages (long-term service → preservation cycles → end of life) and cites well-executed overlays lasting 20–40 years or more regardless of type, with timely intervention adding 20+ years [11, p. 4]. Fiber-reinforced overlays have outperformed comparable plain overlays [1, p. 38].

These are outcomes achieved where the system matched the existing condition, the foundation was stable and drained, the interface behaved as designed, jointing and load transfer were controlled, and construction quality held. They are evidence of potential, not a warranty for a different traffic, climate, or construction context [1].

Monitor condition after opening through the pavement-management program — cracking, faulting, joint load transfer, drainage performance, ride, texture, and repairs all feed preservation timing and the next rehabilitation decision [2], [13], [29].

Life-extension figures differ by source

[1]/[11] cite 20–40 yr; [2] (the preservation guide) is more conservative at 15–30 yr [2, p. 242]. The wider figures come from the overlay-specific guides, which include unbonded overlays performing as new pavements; the narrower ones reflect a preservation-only framing.

Repair and preservation of existing overlays

From [11]: full-depth repair is the most common method (concrete, not asphalt; opening strength 2,000–3,000 psi) [11, p. 15]; partial-depth repair suits thicker overlays (≥7 in.) [11, p. 21]; dowel-bar retrofit for overlays >6 in. [11, p. 22]; cross-stitching down to 6 in. overlays [11, p. 23]. Diamond grinding removes ~¼ in., and most overlays can be ground once or twice within their sacrificial thickness — as-built typically exceeds design by 0.1–0.25 in.; a 0.2 in. loss is roughly a 30% fatigue-life reduction, offset by a 10–15% strength gain from 28 to 90 days [11, p. 25], [11, p. 26]. End of life: place another overlay on top (COC–U design, leaving 4 in. of the existing overlay as support), mill and inlay, or reconstruct [11, p. 28].

Programmatic limitation (Pennsylvania)

The engineering-optimal choice — a cost-effective structural (especially unbonded) overlay on a heavier-traffic route, the case [2] argues for [2, p. 242] — may be ineligible as preservation under Pub. 242. Do not force it into the preservation bucket; route it through 3R/rehabilitation with Pavement Type Selection, a design exception (Pub. 10C §3.4.C, via HDTS), or — for hazardous or rapidly deteriorating sections — the 100%-state-funded waivers in [3, §12.7] (which can waive the 4-in. rigid minimum and the IRI criterion). Coordinate with HDTS early, at scoping.

Related concepts

  • Sub-types — bcoc concrete overlay, ubcoc concrete overlay, bcoa concrete overlay, ubcoa concrete overlay (the four types of Table A.1 / Fig. 11.1).
  • Component — separation layer / geotextile interlayer (unbonded overlays).
  • Prerequisite / complementary — partial depth repair, full-depth repair, slab stabilization; pre-overlay repair drives the bonded-vs-unbonded selection [1, p. 68].
  • Construction control — dowel basket placement checklist for the focused basket inspection workflow [10], [9].
  • Alternative preservation treatments — diamond grinding/grooving, dowel bar retrofit, joint resealing; all on PennDOT's eligible rigid list ([3, §12.2.A.2]).
  • Surface and functional outcome — next generation concrete surface and texture/noise decisions; related to ride and friction, not a substitute for structural design [25], [26].
  • Escalation — rehabilitation / reconstruction, cement-based integrated pavement solutions, or full-depth reclamation where the feasibility gates cannot be closed [15], [16]; where PennDOT files structural overlays ([3, §12.2.C.2]).
  • Parent — pavement preservation.

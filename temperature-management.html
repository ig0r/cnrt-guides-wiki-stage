Temperature Management

Part of: Cracking Mitigation is-subtopic-of
Temperature Management is a topic under Cracking Mitigation.
concrete-temperaturecold-weather-concretinghydrationadmixturesaci-guidelines
Created 2026-09-01 · Updated 2026-09-02

Temperature management in concrete construction is the deliberate control of the thermal environment surrounding fresh and curing concrete to regulate cement hydration rates, maintain workability, and prevent temperature-induced distress. Because cement hydration is an exothermic chemical reaction, elevated temperatures accelerate setting and moisture loss while low temperatures slow reactions and risk freezing before the material sets [1] [2]. Uncontrolled thermal gradients induce internal stresses through expansion and contraction, which can lead to early-age shrinkage cracking or thermal shock if not mitigated [1] [2]. Effective management employs climate-specific strategies such as cooling mixes in hot conditions or heating and insulating in cold conditions, guided by continuous monitoring of ambient factors like wind, humidity, and slab thickness [1] [2].

Key findings

  • Higher ambient temperatures accelerate concrete hydration, leading to faster stiffening and strength gain while increasing the risk of thermal cracking due to expansion and subsequent contraction [1].
  • Concrete hydration effectively halts when temperatures approach freezing (approximately 32°F), necessitating specific cold-weather protection measures [1].
  • Hot-weather conditions, particularly when evaporation rates exceed 0.2 lb/ft²·hr, cause rapid slump loss and plastic shrinkage, requiring mitigation strategies such as fog spraying or the application of approved evaporation reducers [1].
  • Cold-weather placement requires maintaining fresh concrete at or above 50°F for at least 72 hours using methods like heated mixing water, non-chloride accelerators, and insulating blankets [1].
  • Effective temperature management involves continuous monitoring with high accuracy to ensure workability and minimize the risk of thermal cracking [1].

Concrete Thermal Control

Concrete thermal control involves managing the temperature of fresh and curing concrete to ensure proper hydration, workability, and structural integrity [1] [2]. Because cement hydration is an exothermic process, ambient and mix temperatures directly influence reaction rates, setting times, and strength development [1] [2]. Extreme heat accelerates reactions and moisture loss, increasing the risk of shrinkage cracking, while cold conditions slow hydration and may lead to freezing before the concrete sets [1] [2]. Effective management requires balancing these thermal effects through environmental controls and material adjustments to maintain the concrete within an optimal temperature range during placement and early curing [1] [2].

This dormancy stage allows for workability before the exothermic reactions accelerate, which is a critical factor in managing temperature-induced distress during construction.

Research problem — Uncontrolled concrete temperatures, ranging from excessive heat to sub-freezing conditions, create critical risks such as rapid slump loss, premature stiffening, and early-age shrinkage cracking that compromise workability and long-term structural performance [1]. Extreme ambient temperatures alter the physical and chemical behavior of concrete mixtures, leading to accelerated strength gain or delayed hydration that increases susceptibility to plastic and thermal cracking as well as freeze damage [2]. The need for formal temperature management programs arises from the requirement to maintain concrete within specific thermal limits to prevent costly rework, premature pavement failure, and defects in surface finish and durability [1] [2].

State of the art — Current best practice integrates continuous temperature monitoring with proactive thermal control strategies tailored to specific weather conditions, ensuring fresh concrete remains within an optimal range of 50°F to 60°F [1]. In hot weather, practitioners mitigate excessive heat and evaporation through measures such as night placement, shading, precooled aggregates, and the use of low-heat supplementary cementitious materials or retarding admixtures [1] [2]. Cold weather protocols prioritize maintaining concrete temperatures above 50°F for at least 72 hours using insulated blankets, heated mix components, and approved accelerating admixtures while strictly prohibiting chlorides [1] [2].

Reproducibility is ensured by mandating strict adherence to specific weather thresholds and requiring continuous monitoring of fresh concrete temperature alongside hourly evaporation rate reporting for designated projects [2].

Implications — Designers and contractors must implement proactive thermal management strategies, such as scheduling pours during cooler hours or using chilled materials in hot weather, to counteract the accelerating effects of ambient heat on concrete setting times [1]. Specifying adequate heating and insulation is essential in cold conditions to prevent hydration from halting, ensuring that concrete maintains sufficient temperature during its critical early curing phase [1]. Continuous monitoring of concrete temperature and evaporation rates is required to trigger timely interventions like fog spraying or the application of evaporation reducers, thereby preventing plastic shrinkage and thermal cracking that can compromise structural integrity and delay project schedules [1].

Limitations — Cooling strategies are constrained by the requirement that total water and ice additions must not exceed the original mix design specifications [1]. Hot-weather control measures become unreliable under high evaporation rates, necessitating more aggressive curing or stopping paving operations entirely when environmental conditions are too severe [1]. Cold-weather protocols require extended temperature maintenance and acknowledge that strength development is slower with supplementary cementitious materials, potentially delaying subsequent construction activities [1].

Surface Textures

surfacetextures
Created 2026-09-01 · Updated 2026-09-02

Surface texture refers to the intentional physical irregularities on a pavement’s top layer that govern how tires interact with the road surface. These small-scale features form shallow channels and peaks that allow water beneath tires to escape, reducing hydrostatic pressure and preventing hydroplaning while also providing contact points for traction [1]. Texture is commonly classified by the size of its features into microtexture, macrotexture, and megatexture, each defined by characteristic wavelength and vertical amplitude ranges [2]. These textures affect key performance aspects such as wet-weather friction, hydroplaning resistance, noise generation, rolling resistance, and tire wear [3] [2].

Surface Textures define the critical interface between tire and pavement by establishing physical irregularities that directly govern friction, noise generation, and hydroplaning resistance to ensure roadway safety and durability. Macrotexture provides the deep channels necessary for water evacuation during wet conditions, preventing hydroplaning. Microtexture creates the fine-scale roughness required for high-frequency tire-pavement interaction, enhancing dry grip and reducing rolling resistance. Longitudinal grooves guide water away from the contact patch along the direction of travel, maintaining stability under braking. Transverse patterns disrupt water flow across the lane width, improving lateral traction during cornering. Open-graded friction courses offer a highly porous structure that absorbs tire noise and provides exceptional skid resistance through aggregate interlock.

Key findings

  • Demonstrated that designers must balance providing sufficient friction for safety with limiting tire-pavement noise, as this trade-off underpins pavement sustainability [3].
  • Found that longitudinal tining applied in the plastic stage is now favored because it preserves required skid resistance while cutting the characteristic whine associated with older transverse patterns [3].
  • Observed that post-cure treatments such as diamond grinding or next-generation concrete surface patterns achieve high friction, lower noise, and deliberately generate negative macrotexture beneficial for heavily trafficked roads [3].
  • Reported that uniform application of any texture technique is essential because consistency governs both friction and acoustic outcomes regardless of the method used [3].
  • Identified abrasion resistance as the key factor in preserving texture over a pavement’s service life, driven by strong aggregates, high compressive-strength mixes, densifying finishes, and adequate curing [3].
  • Showed that skid resistance is largely independent of texture type, whereas noise varies markedly with diamond-ground surfaces being the quietest and transverse tined surfaces the loudest [3].
  • Evaluated that all approved airport pavement texturing methods aim to create channels that relieve hydrostatic pressure and provide traction, but differ in aggressiveness, equipment requirements, and debris handling [1].
  • Recommended avoiding coarse patterns exceeding one-inch spacing and favoring down-oriented textures such as longitudinal grooves over upward fins to maintain consistent friction characteristics [2].

Texture Geometry Design and Performance Trade-offs

Evidence: 3 reports (3 primary)

Surface texture geometry is engineered to balance competing performance requirements, primarily providing sufficient friction for safety while managing the acoustic impact of tire-pavement interaction [3]. The design involves selecting specific feature scales and orientations that create shallow channels for water drainage to prevent hydroplaning, alongside peaks that ensure traction under wet conditions [1]. Trade-offs in texture selection arise because different geometric patterns and production methods yield varying levels of noise generation and skid resistance, requiring a compromise between quietness and safety performance [3] [2].

Research problem — Engineers must resolve the fundamental trade-off between maximizing friction for wet-weather safety and minimizing tire-pavement noise while preserving long-term durability [3]. There is a need to determine precise timing and methods for applying texturing on airport pavements to ensure adequate skid resistance and hydroplaning protection without compromising structural integrity [1]. The field must address how insufficient microtexture, excessive macrotexture, or improper pattern orientation degrade wet-weather friction and increase rolling resistance [2].

State of the art — The industry has converged on longitudinal tining as the default finish because it preserves sufficient tire-pavement friction while generating less noise than older transverse methods [3]. Design practice balances multi-scale texture requirements by preserving microtexture for dry adhesion and specifying macrotexture to channel water and reduce hydroplaning risk without compromising ride quality or acoustic performance [2].

Novelty & rigor — Research is shifting from traditional positive macrotexture toward designs that simultaneously optimize skid resistance, noise reduction, and cost efficiency through innovations like negative macrotexture and orientation-based tining [3]. Reproducible methods now emphasize precise control over texturing timing and rate during the plastic concrete phase using specialized tools, alongside standardized verification protocols that replace traditional sand patch tests with manufactured glass spheres for improved uniformity [1].

Across the reviewed reports, texture geometry design is fundamentally constrained by the need to balance high skid resistance with noise mitigation, leading to a consensus that longitudinal or downward-oriented patterns are preferred over transverse or upward-facing ones to reduce acoustic impact while maintaining traction. While specific geometric parameters such as channel depth and spacing vary by application context, all reports converge on the principle that uniform texture application is critical for consistent performance, with deviations in pattern orientation or spacing directly influencing both friction stability and noise generation. Implications — Designers should prioritize texture geometries that simultaneously enhance friction and reduce noise, such as longitudinal tining or diamond grinding, while avoiding uniform transverse patterns that generate excessive audible whine [3]. Specification of cut-groove patterns must enforce tight spacing and randomized orientations to prevent uneven stress concentrations and ensure consistent tire-road contact without compromising durability [2]. Construction practices require disciplined quality control synchronized with concrete bleed-water conditions, as relying on specific texturing tools is less effective than ensuring proper timing to avoid premature or uneven texture formation [1].

The following figure illustrates how these different texturing methods impact acoustic performance.

Distribution of noise levels from various texturing methods [3]

Although the distribution of noise levels is fairly large, in general, diamond-ground pavements are the quietest, followed by drag textures, longitudinal tined, and transverse tined.

Limitations — Achieving the intended safety and acoustic benefits is highly sensitive to execution quality, as deviations in placement or finishing can significantly degrade texture durability and performance [3]. The difficulty of detecting bleed water in modern mix designs complicates timing for texturing operations, particularly under environmental conditions that accelerate moisture loss [1]. There is no mandatory quantitative verification process to ensure produced textures meet specific design standards, leaving performance outcomes dependent on subjective assessment and trial protocols [1].

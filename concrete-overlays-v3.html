Concrete Overlays

Primary source: the Guide to Concrete Overlays, 4th ed. [1] is the primary technical voice here; the specialized overlay guides fill individual facets, [5] supplies the preservation positioning, and PennDOT Publication 242, Ch. 12 [8] supplies the Pennsylvania eligibility layer (cited by section number). Cross-guide disagreements are called out in place.

Overview

Definition & purpose — A concrete overlay is a layer of portland cement concrete (PCC) placed over an existing asphalt, composite, or concrete pavement [1, p. 2]. Overlays are grouped into four types by substrate and interface — concrete-on-asphalt bonded/unbonded (COA–B, COA–U) and concrete-on-concrete bonded/unbonded (COC–B, COC–U) [1, p. 5]. In a bonded overlay the two layers act as one monolithic slab with a single neutral axis, lowering flexural stress and deflection; in an unbonded overlay the layers bend independently, each with its own neutral axis [1, p. 16], [1, p. 81]. Between 2000 and 2017, 71% of US concrete overlays were on asphalt and 29% on concrete [1, p. 5]; at least 46 states built ~1,289 overlays through 2017 [1, p. 3]. The purpose is to preserve or rehabilitate while retaining the equity in the existing pavement [1, p. 36].

Classification is contested — thin "preservation" vs. thick "rehabilitation"

[1] itself splits overlays by thickness: thin COC overlays (2–4 in.) are a preservation strategy and ≥6 in. overlays a rehabilitation strategy [1, p. 36]. [5] (Ch. 11) instead places overlays — including thicker unbonded ones that "add structural capacity" — in the preservation toolbox and argues against that ≤4 in. cutoff [5, p. 242], even while quoting the FHWA definition that preservation "do[es] not add capacity or structural value" [5, p. 8]. PennDOT Pub. 242 sides with the thickness/structural split (see the Pennsylvania callouts below). So whether a given overlay "is preservation" depends on the governing jurisdiction, not on the treatment.

Application

When to use / candidates — Type selection follows existing condition, traffic, geometry, and desired life [1, p. 4]; the existing pavement is rated good/fair/poor/deteriorated by a six-step evaluation [1, p. 8]. Every project must be cored (2–4 cores/lane-mile by functional class) to verify thickness/condition and check asphalt stripping, with FWD for support and GPR for layer thickness [1, p. 13].

  • COA–U — asphalt/composite with significant HMA deterioration (rutting, potholes, alligator cracking, shoving, pumping) [1, p. 27]; needs ≥3–4 in. of sound asphalt as a construction platform [1, p. 28].
  • COA–B — asphalt/composite in fair-or-better structural condition; ≥3 in. sound asphalt for bond [1, p. 30], [1, p. 82].
  • COC–U — existing concrete in poor-to-deteriorated condition (even with slow-acting MRD) provided support is stable and uniform; ≥4 in. concrete platform [1, p. 36], [1, p. 38].
  • COC–B — existing concrete in good-to-excellent condition with remaining life ≥ the overlay's; a narrowly applicable, infrequent case [1, p. 20], [1, p. 114], [6, p. 3].
  • Thin (≤5 in.) overlays are a preservation technique for concrete pavements with deteriorated joints; the key is milling off the joint deterioration before overlaying [2, p. 1].
  • Parking lots / low-volume — COA–B for low-to-medium-severity distress, COA–U for high-severity over stable support [7, p. 1], [7, p. 11].
  • ~75% of overlays are unbonded, ~25% bonded across >1,000 projects [3, p. 2].
When NOT to use
  • No bonded overlay where the substrate has significant structural deterioration, inadequate base/subbase, or asphalt stripping/delamination — use unbonded instead [1, p. 25], [1, p. 30].
  • No concrete overlay over fast-acting MRD (fast D-cracking or ASR) [2, p. 2].
  • No COC–B where joints show MRD, poor support/drainage, vertical-clearance limits, or numerous random cracks hard to reproduce in the overlay [1, p. 115], [4, p. 2].
  • Never use concrete shoulders with a COA–B (edge support promotes bond loss) [1, p. 96].
  • If, after milling, extensive partial-/full-depth repairs would still be required, the pavement is not a good bonded candidate (may still suit unbonded) [2, p. 2].
Pennsylvania — what counts as preservation (PennDOT Pub. 242, Ch. 12)

On a PennDOT project, eligibility is governed by Pub. 242, and it diverges from [1]/[5] exactly on structural overlays:

  • A preservation project shall not "increase the structural depth of the existing pavement to increase pavement life"; a first-time concrete-pavement overlay must meet the minimum depths of Tables 9.5/10.4 ([8, §12.2.C.2]). Appendix A, Table 1 classes a "structural overlay" as rehabilitation.
  • PennDOT's eligible rigid-preservation list contains no concrete-on-concrete overlay; the only concrete overlay it admits as preservation is an ultra-thin bonded concrete overlay of asphalt, as a non-structural wearing course ([8, §12.2.A.1.o]).
  • So an unbonded (or thick bonded) overlay is not preservation in PA — it is rehabilitation/3R, requiring a full pavement design and Pavement Type Selection, not the "no design required" preservation path ([8, §12.3]). Document the structural-vs-non-structural rationale in the Pavement Type Selection Memo ([8, §12.5]), since a reviewer reading [1]/[5] may expect overlays to qualify.

Implementation

Materials

  • Cementitious — Type I/II (ASTM C150), blended (C595), or hydraulic (C1157); Type III not recommended (shrinkage cracking) [1, p. 44]. SCM replacement typically 15–35%, and 20–40% at 65–75 °F mixture temperature [1, p. 44], [1, p. 46].
  • Aggregate (ASTM C33) — maximum coarse-aggregate size ≤ ⅓ of the overlay thickness [1, p. 44]. w/cm typically 0.40–0.45; paste volume ≤25% for shrinkage control [1, p. 45], [1, p. 46].
  • Macrofibers — synthetic 3–8 lb/yd³ or steel 25–75 lb/yd³ (≈0.2–0.5% by volume); improve post-cracking toughness, giving effective flexural strength f_eff = MOR + f_150 (ASTM C1609, f_150 typ. 100–200 psi). Fibers do not replace dowels for faulting; microfibers give no structural capacity [1, p. 47], [1, p. 49]. Fiber reinforcement is recommended particularly for overlays ≤4 in. [2, p. 13].
  • Tie/dowel bars — dowels (ASTM A1078) not used below ~7 in.; tie bars Grade 60 No. 4 typical, 30 in. spacing, not recommended below 5 in. overlay [1, p. 51].
  • Full-depth-repair concrete designed for 3,000 psi at 28 days; deteriorated-joint fill uses 2,000 psi flowable mortar [1, p. 69], [1, p. 40].
  • COC–B only — overlay aggregate CTE and modulus should match or be lower than the existing pavement to limit debonding stress (asphalt E ≈ 400,000 psi vs concrete ≈ 4,000,000 psi) [1, p. 44], [1, p. 82].

Separation layer / interlayer (unbonded overlays)

An unbonded concrete-on-concrete overlay requires a separation layer; over asphalt (COA–U) it usually needs none [4, p. 3], [1, p. 19]. The layer must balance three functions: isolation (a shear plane that relieves stress and stops reflective cracking), drainage, and bedding (a cushion reducing curling/warping and keying) [9, p. 3]. Two materials dominate — a nominal 1–2 in. asphalt layer or a nonwoven geotextile [1, p. 50], [2, p. 14]. Asphalt layers can strip and cause loss of support; geotextile eliminates that risk and speeds construction [9, p. 3].

Cross-guide discrepancy — geotextile mass by thickness

The authoritative interlayer source ([9], corroborated by [3] and [2]) specifies 13.3 oz/yd² (<5 in.) and 14.7 oz/yd² (≥5 in.) [9, p. 4], [3, p. 37], [2, p. 15]. [1] rounds these to 13.0 oz/yd² @130 mils and 15.0 oz/yd² @170 mils [1, p. 50]. Use the 13.3 / 14.7 spec; the difference is a rounding in the 4th-ed. guide, not a real design change. Field practice trends thinner under thin overlays (MnDOT is evaluating 8 oz/yd² under a 3 in. overlay) [9, p. 6].

Equipment

  • Milling / removal — carbide cold-planing machines; depth by stringline, ski, or 3D control [1, p. 69], [1, p. 76]. Heavy overlay removal wants ≥800–1,000 hp machines with a coarse-cutting drum and concrete-specific carbide teeth [6, p. 31].
  • Pavers — conventional slipform, stringless (3D), and zero-clearance/zero-offset pavers, some with dowel-bar inserters; stringless needs only 2–3 ft lateral clearance vs 6–10 ft for stringline [1, p. 60], [1, p. 72].
  • Saws — early-entry lightweight saws let crews on sooner; extra saws needed for short joint spacing [1, p. 33], [1, p. 74].
  • Cleaning / surface prep — sweeper or air blower; ~150 psi compressed air to clean deteriorated joints; COC–B requires shotblasting (most effective) to remove milling microcracking [1, p. 70], [1, p. 118].
  • Geotextile securing — adhesive spray or pins/nails through 2.0–2.75 in. galvanized discs at ≤6 ft spacing [1, p. 71], [9, p. 7].

Procedure (construction sequence)

  1. Pre-overlay repairs — limited for unbonded (only distresses that would reflect through); more extensive for COA–B to restore fair/good condition; rocking slabs get full-depth repair [1, p. 68], [1, p. 69].
  2. Milling (asphalt surfaces) — optional; remove non-structural distress/rutting, expose sound asphalt, add bond texture; if base is exposed, mill 2–3 in. extra and thicken the overlay the same amount (tell the sawing crew) [1, p. 68].
  3. Deteriorated-joint repair (COC–U) — clean with hand tools + ~150 psi air, fill flush with flowable mortar before the separation layer [1, p. 70].
  4. Separation layer (COC–U) — asphalt (control in-place air voids) or geotextile: mill faulting >0.25 in., unroll taut and wrinkle-free, overlap 6–10 in. with ≤3 layers at any point, daylight to the edge/underdrain, secure by adhesive or pins ≤6 ft o.c.; place fabric ≤2–3 days before paving [1, p. 70], [1, p. 71], [9, p. 7].
  5. Placement — sweep; keep the surface damp with no free water; wet/cool if ≥120 °F; bid concrete by volume (yd³), placement by area (yd²) [1, p. 72], [1, p. 29].
  6. Curing — white-pigmented curing compound on surface and vertical faces immediately after texturing; ~150 ft²/gal in two coats for overlays <7 in. [1, p. 74].
  7. Sawing — timely, T/3, through the slab edge; extra saws for short spacing [1, p. 75].
  8. Sealing / filling — per type and climate; low-speed (≤45 mph) roads designed with filled joints flush to the surface [1, p. 75], [6, p. 27].
  9. Open to traffic — per opening-strength criteria (maturity method) [1, p. 41], [7, p. 3].

COC–B swaps in shotblast surface prep and matched joint sawing (over each existing joint), with no grout coat [1, p. 117], [1, p. 118].

Environmental & operational limits
  • When the asphalt/separation-layer surface is ≥120 °F (49 °C), wet/cool it and keep it free of standing water to reduce early-age cracking [1, p. 29], [2, p. 22].
  • COC–B must not be placed when the mixture and existing-pavement temperatures differ by more than 35 °F; hot, dry, windy, low-humidity conditions are worst (evaporation → debonding) [1, p. 119].
  • Thin unbonded overlays have a shorter sawing window (higher surface-area-to-volume ratio); keep early wheel loads 1–2 ft off free edges [1, p. 37], [1, p. 75].
  • Cold weather: SCMs slow set — use blankets, heated mixtures, or SCM-free mixes [1, p. 47].

Quality control & acceptance

  • Typical acceptance items: thickness, strength, air content, combined gradation, smoothness [1, p. 5]. Cured surface (and vertical faces) must appear uniformly white "like a sheet of paper," free of streaks [1, p. 74].
  • Dowel placement verified after paving (MIT-SCAN, GPR, coring); suspend paving if baskets move [1, p. 73]. COC–B bond strength typically specified 100–200 psi [1, p. 94].
  • Opening strength by the maturity method / ASTM C1609 residual strength; HIPERPAV for early-age cracking and sawing-window planning [1, p. 75].
  • Air after placement ≥5% (≈5% strength loss per 1% air); sample hardened air if loss through the paver approaches 3% [7, p. 57].
Pennsylvania — verification & submission (PennDOT Pub. 242, Ch. 12)

PennDOT's pre-work verification also gates eligibility: FWD to confirm failed joints and <10% patching ([8, §12.3.B]), coring for milling depth ([8, §12.3.A.2]), IRI + friction data, a Scoping Field View ([8, §12.4]), and a Pavement Type Selection Memo to HDTS ([8, §12.5]). These are the auditable form of [1]'s mandatory-coring / uniform-support requirement.

Performance & service life

Overlays commonly deliver 20-year low-maintenance service lives, and many reach 30–40 years [1, p. 2]. By type: COA–U has 30+ years of good-to-excellent performance in many states [1, p. 27]; COC–U has been used 40+ years with life comparable to a new full-depth pavement [1, p. 36]; COC–B extends life 20–25 years (some >35), with a 15-year minimum before maintenance [1, p. 114]. [6] frames three performance stages (long-term service → preservation cycles → end of life) and cites well-executed overlays lasting 20–40 years or more regardless of type, with timely intervention adding 20+ years [6, p. 4]. Fiber-reinforced overlays have outperformed comparable plain overlays [1, p. 38].

Life-extension figures differ by source

[1]/[6] cite 20–40 yr; [5] (the preservation guide) is more conservative at 15–30 yr [5, p. 242]. The wider figures come from the overlay-specific guides, which include unbonded overlays performing as new pavements; the narrower ones reflect a preservation-only framing.

Repair & preservation of existing overlays ([6]): full-depth repair is the most common method (concrete, not asphalt; opening strength 2,000–3,000 psi) [6, p. 15]; partial-depth repair suits thicker overlays (≥7 in.) [6, p. 21]; dowel-bar retrofit for overlays >6 in. [6, p. 22]; cross-stitching down to 6 in. overlays [6, p. 23]; diamond grinding removes ~¼ in. and most overlays can be ground once or twice within their sacrificial thickness (as-built typically exceeds design 0.1–0.25 in.; a 0.2 in. loss ≈ 30% fatigue-life reduction, offset by 10–15% strength gain from 28→90 days) [6, p. 25], [6, p. 26]. End-of-life: place another overlay on top (COC–U design, leaving 4 in. of existing overlay as support), mill and inlay, or reconstruct [6, p. 28].

Programmatic limitation (Pennsylvania)

The engineering-optimal choice — a cost-effective structural (esp. unbonded) overlay on a heavier-traffic route, the case [5] argues for [5, p. 242] — may be ineligible as preservation under Pub. 242. Do not force it into the preservation bucket; route it through 3R/rehabilitation with Pavement Type Selection, a design exception (Pub. 10C §3.4.C, via HDTS), or — for hazardous/rapidly deteriorating sections — the 100%-state-funded waivers in [8, §12.7] (which can waive the 4-in. rigid minimum and the IRI criterion). Coordinate with HDTS early, at scoping.

Related concepts

  • Sub-types — bcoc concrete overlay, ubcoc concrete overlay, bcoa concrete overlay, ubcoa concrete overlay (the four types of Table A.1 / Fig. 11.1).
  • Component — separation layer / geotextile interlayer (unbonded overlays).
  • Alternative preservation treatments — diamond grinding/grooving, dowel bar retrofit, joint resealing; all on PennDOT's eligible rigid list ([8, §12.2.A.2]).
  • Prerequisite / complementary — partial depth repair, full-depth repair, slab stabilization (pre-overlay repair drives bonded-vs-unbonded selection) [1, p. 68].
  • Escalation — rehabilitation / reconstruction; where PennDOT files structural overlays ([8, §12.2.C.2]).
  • Parent — pavement preservation.

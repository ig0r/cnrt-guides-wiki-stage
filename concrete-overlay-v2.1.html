Concrete Overlays

Use this page for: selecting, designing, constructing, or accepting a concrete overlay over an existing asphalt, composite, or concrete pavement. This is a practitioner-facing synthesis, not a substitute for project-specific structural design, agency specifications, or a pavement evaluation.

Overview

Definition and purpose

A concrete overlay is a new concrete pavement layer placed over an existing asphalt, composite, or concrete pavement to restore function and extend service life while retaining useful parts of the existing structure. The principal overlay family is selected from the existing pavement material and the intended interface: concrete-on-asphalt bonded (COA-B), concrete-on-asphalt unbonded (COA-U), concrete-on-concrete bonded (COC-B), or concrete-on-concrete unbonded (COC-U). [1], [2]

The system decision is not merely a surface-treatment choice. A bonded overlay uses composite action and therefore depends on a sound, prepared interface; an unbonded overlay uses the existing pavement as a foundation and must have uniform support, drainage, and a compatible separation strategy where the underlying pavement is concrete. [1], [3]

Key characteristics

Overlay familyExisting pavement and interfacePrincipal valueMain condition to verify
COA-BGood-quality asphalt; concrete is bonded to asphaltCan capitalize on the structural value of a sound asphalt layerBond and asphalt condition must be reliable. [1]

The primary structural concern is uniformity of support, not simply a high support value. An overlay can be designed for a range of support strengths, but abrupt changes in support can cause distress. Drainage, rocking slabs, broken concrete, joints, localized weak areas, and pavement edge conditions are therefore selection inputs rather than construction afterthoughts. [1], [11]

Application

When to use a concrete overlay

Use an overlay when the existing pavement can contribute stable, reasonably uniform support after economically justified repairs and the project can accommodate the added elevation, drainage, tie-in, utility, curb, and traffic-control changes. A feasibility study should combine records review, distress mapping, field inspection, and targeted coring or testing; it should not select an overlay family from a single visual rating. [1], [6], [16]

Concrete overlays are especially appropriate where reconstruction would discard a usable foundation, where durability and lower-maintenance service are desired, or where expedited staged construction is valuable. The primary overlay guide reports many overlays with low-maintenance lives of at least 20 years and documents longer-performing examples, but the page treats those as experience, not a project warranty. [1]

When not to use one yet

Do not proceed directly to an overlay when any of the following is unresolved:

  • Existing support is nonuniform or unstable, indicated by rocking slabs, severe breakup, widespread loss of support, or abrupt structural transitions. Investigate and repair the cause first. [1], [11]
  • Drainage cannot carry water away from the pavement system. An overlay alone does not correct a drainage defect. [1], [6]
  • The selected bonded system cannot achieve a dependable bond because the existing pavement is not in suitable condition or because surface preparation, jointing, mixture, and weather controls cannot be delivered. [1], [3]
  • Required grade changes create unresolved conflicts at bridges, curbs, gutters, drainage inlets, utilities, sidewalks, or ADA routes. Parking-lot work commonly exposes these fixed-elevation constraints. [1], [7]
  • The condition is so widespread that full-depth reconstruction, full-depth reclamation with cement, or another rehabilitation strategy is more appropriate. [22], [23]

Decision path: select an overlay system

  1. Establish the candidate condition. Review history, layer data, traffic, drainage, distresses, and field evidence; core or test where the condition or layer structure is uncertain. [1], [6], [16]
  2. Resolve foundation and drainage defects. If support will remain nonuniform after practical repairs, pause the overlay decision and evaluate reconstruction or foundation rehabilitation. [1], [11]
  3. Classify the existing pavement. Existing asphalt/composite leads to a COA decision; existing concrete leads to a COC decision. [1]
  4. Decide whether composite bond is dependable. For COA-B, the asphalt must be good or economically restorable to good condition. For COC-B, the existing concrete must be good to excellent and the project must control surface preparation, bonding, and joint compatibility. If those conditions are not met, evaluate the unbonded counterpart. [1], [3]
  5. For COC-U, choose a separation-layer strategy. Asphalt and nonwoven geotextile are common choices. Evaluate drainage, existing-pavement condition, traffic, profile, material behavior, and constructability; do not specify fabric solely by generic weight. [1], [5], [3]
  6. Complete structural design and constructability review. Determine thickness, jointing, load transfer, fiber use, shoulder/edge details, staging, and opening criteria using the governing agency method. The selection page does not replace the overlay design procedure. [2], [1]

Equipment

Plan conventional concrete-paving equipment plus the equipment needed for the selected system: survey/machine-control tools; milling, removal, and repair equipment; cleaning and surface-preparation equipment; separation-layer placement equipment; approved dowel-basket handling and anchoring equipment; batch, transport, placement, consolidation, finishing, texturing, curing, and saw-cutting equipment. Verify the equipment plan in a pre-paving meeting and use the project quality plan to assign inspection responsibility. [1], [8], [9]

Procedure

  1. Evaluate and document the existing pavement. Assemble records, map distresses, identify drainage and elevation constraints, inspect support, and collect cores or tests where needed. Make the repair scope visible in the plans and quality plan. [1], [6], [16]
  2. Select and design the overlay system. Choose COA-B, COA-U, COC-B, or COC-U only after condition and interface requirements are verified. Size the overlay, jointing, load transfer, and fibers by the governing design method. [2], [1]
  3. Execute and accept pre-overlay repairs. Mill or remove unsuitable asphalt, repair localized support and subbase problems, repair concrete joints or localized deterioration as needed, and prevent isolated repairs from creating new abrupt support changes. [4], [15], [11]
  4. Prepare the interface. Clean and prepare bonded interfaces to the project requirements. For unbonded concrete-over-concrete work, place and protect the selected separation layer; for asphalt interfaces, correct profile and instability before paving. [3], [1]
  5. Establish geometry and load-transfer details. Verify grade control, joint layout, basket location and anchorage, tie bars, and planned saw-cut sequence before the first load of concrete. Use a formal basket inspection where dowels are specified. [12], [9]
  6. Place, consolidate, finish, texture, and cure. Apply conventional concrete-paving controls, preserve alignment and thickness, and cure immediately after texturing. Select texture for project friction/noise goals and verify that it is compatible with the overlay system. [1], [8], [18], [19]
  7. Saw, protect, and open to traffic. Cut joints at the planned time, protect early-age concrete from damaging traffic, and open only when the project-specific strength/maturity and operational criteria have been met. [1], [8], [21]

Environmental and operational limits

Bonded overlays require particular attention to the existing surface, concrete mixture, jointing, and weather because bond loss can lead quickly to cracking. Unbonded systems still require a dry, stable, properly drained interface; a separation layer is not a remedy for unstable support or trapped water. [1], [3], [5]

Set project-specific limits for temperature, wind, precipitation, curing, saw-cut timing, concrete delivery, and opening strength in the contract documents and quality plan. The guides provide construction practices and acceptance controls, but this page does not infer universal numerical weather or opening limits from them. [8], [9], [10]

Release-to-placement checklist

Use this as a field hold-point list; agency specifications and the approved quality plan remain controlling.

  • Existing-pavement evaluation, design basis, repair limits, and drainage details are available to the field team. [1], [6]
  • Required milling, removals, partial-depth/full-depth repairs, and localized foundation repairs are accepted; no rocking slabs, open voids, or unaddressed unstable areas remain. [4], [15], [11]
  • The bonded surface is prepared and clean, or the unbonded separation layer is the approved type, continuous, protected, and free of standing water. [3], [5]
  • Grade, cross slope, tie-ins, drainage inlets, utilities, and fixed-elevation constraints are checked. [1], [7]
  • Dowel baskets, tie bars, and joint layout are inspected for location, elevation, alignment, anchorage, and damage; deviations are dispositioned before paving. [12], [9], [20]
  • The approved mixture, trial-batch results when fibers are used, material certificates, curing compound, and test equipment are ready. [1], [27], [21]
  • Paving, curing, saw-cutting, traffic control, inspection staffing, and nonconformance response are coordinated in the pre-paving plan. [8], [9], [10]

Performance and service life

Overlay performance depends on the design system matching the existing pavement condition, a stable and drained foundation, reliable interface behavior, controlled jointing/load transfer, and construction quality. The primary guide reports experience with low-maintenance lives of at least 20 years and examples lasting 30 to 40 years; such outcomes are evidence of potential, not a promise for a different traffic, climate, or construction context. [1]

For bonded overlays, maintain the bond and control reflective cracking risk. For unbonded overlays, preserve uniform support and the performance of the separation layer. Asphalt separation layers that strip or consolidate, and geotextiles that are unsuitable for the system, can affect faulting, cracking, and ride. [1], [5]

Monitor condition after opening through the pavement-management program: cracking, faulting, joint load transfer, drainage performance, ride, texture, and repairs are useful inputs to preservation timing and the next rehabilitation decision. [6], [29], [14]

Symptom or riskLikely system issueFirst response

Faulting, cracking, or loss of ride near localized weak areasNonuniform support, drainage distress, rocking/broken slabs, or unresolved base/subgrade defects.Diagnose support and drainage; repair the cause and reassess the overlay structural design if the condition is widespread. [1], [11]

Geotextile-related constructability or performance concernUnsuitable material/thickness, poor overlap/detailing, hot or wet fabric, or application outside the intended system.Recheck product requirements, placement details, surface condition, and system applicability before placing concrete. [1], [5]
Joint distress or poor load transferIncompatible joint layout, delayed/inadequate sawing, damaged/misaligned baskets, or a dowel decision mismatched to traffic and thickness.Inspect the joint system, basket records, and early-age construction sequence. Macrofibers do not replace dowels for faulting control. [1], [12], [13]

Related concepts

  • Prerequisite: Evaluate existing pavement condition for records review, distress mapping, field inspection, and targeted testing before selecting an overlay. [6], [16]
  • Decision support: Overlay option decision example demonstrates how to record the decision logic and evidence boundary.
  • Complementary repair: Partial-Depth Repair addresses localized near-surface concrete deterioration that can be part of pre-overlay repair scope. [15]
  • Construction control: Dowel Basket Placement Checklist provides the focused inspection workflow for projects using baskets. [12], [9]
  • Surface and functional outcome: Diamond Grooving and Next Generation Concrete Surface relate to texture, ride, friction, and noise decisions; they are not a substitute for overlay structural design. [18], [19]
  • Alternative strategy: Cement-based integrated pavement solutions and full-depth reclamation may be better candidates when the overlay feasibility gates cannot be satisfied. [22], [23]

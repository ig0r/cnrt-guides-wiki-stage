Overlay Thickness Optimization

Part of: Overlay Thickness Calculation is-subtopic-of
Overlay Thickness Optimization is a topic under Overlay Thickness Calculation.
overlay-designthickness-optimizationload-transfer-coefficientdesign-lifeserviceability-standards
Created 2026-09-09 · Updated 2026-09-12

Overlay Thickness Optimization is a deterministic design procedure that determines the thinnest viable concrete overlay capable of sustaining projected traffic loads and service life while satisfying structural and durability requirements [1]. It operates by feeding a chosen design life, material properties (concrete modulus of rupture, elastic modulus), subgrade reaction modulus, and the load‑transfer coefficient J—derived from shoulder type and dowel usage—into the AASHTO slab‑design equations to compute the required full‑depth slab thickness, from which the overlay thickness is obtained by subtracting the effective existing slab depth (linear or square‑root form) and iterating until constraints are met [1]. A defining characteristic is the practical lower bound of approximately seven inches when dowels are employed, ensuring adequate concrete cover over the bars and preventing vibrator interference, while adjustments to J can reduce the required thickness by about one inch per unit change [1].

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay Thickness Optimization is used to resolve the engineering need of sizing an overlay so that it can sustain increased traffic loading and longer service periods without over‑designing the structure. By quantifying how changes in design life, material properties, subgrade stiffness, or load‑transfer coefficient affect the required slab thickness, designers can adjust these parameters to achieve a structurally adequate yet thinner overlay while still providing sufficient concrete cover and effective load transfer. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 37–65)

Overlay Thickness Optimization must satisfy structural and durability objectives by ensuring the overlay provides sufficient concrete cover over dowels—“dow\nels are not recommended for overlay thicknesses less than 7 in because the overlay would not provide enough concrete cover above the bars and the use of vibrators in the concrete may interfere with the bars”. This minimum‑cover requirement also fulfills safety goals, preventing construction equipment from damaging reinforcement. Functional objectives related to load transfer can be met by adjusting the load‑transfer coefficient, as “a change in load transfer coefficient, J, will decrease the required overlay thickness by approximately 1.0 in (with rounded thicknesses changing from 8.0 to 7.0 in)”, which “allows reducing the overlay thickness to 7 in”. By adhering to these constraints while achieving the slab thickness dictated by structural calculations, the design also meets serviceability requirements for long‑term performance. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 48–52)

Scope and applicability

Overlay Thickness Optimization is appropriate for unbonded concrete overlays that use dowels for load transfer, especially when tied‑concrete shoulders replace asphalt shoulders (or vice‑versa). It also applies where 1.5‑inch dowel bars spaced at 12 in are installed, permitting the overlay thickness to be reduced to about 7 in while still providing sufficient concrete cover. The approach enables designers to adjust overlay dimensions in response to changes in the load transfer coefficient caused by shoulder material changes or dowel installation. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 48–65)

“When an overlay would need to be thinner than about seven inches, using dowels is impractical because there would not be sufficient concrete cover over the bars and vibrators could interfere with the bars; under those conditions a different design approach should be considered.” Likewise, “When the use of dowels would produce an overlay thinner than about seven inches, the practical limits on concrete cover and bar interference render the overlay‑thickness optimization approach inappropriate, so a different design method should be considered.” Even though lowering the load transfer coefficient can reduce thickness by roughly one inch—“a change in load transfer coefficient, J, will decrease the required overlay thickness by approximately 1.0 in. (with rounded thicknesses changing from 8.0 to 7.0 in.)”—the minimum viable thickness for dowel installation remains seven inches; if calculations indicate a thinner layer (e.g., five inches), another strategy must be adopted. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 48–52)

Design inputs and requirements

The design of overlay thickness is governed primarily by the 1993 AASHTO Guide, specifically “table 2.6 in Part II of the 1993 AASHTO Guide” which defines the load‑transfer coefficient (J) used in slab‑thickness calculations. Adjusting J from higher to lower values results in a thinner overlay because “a change in load transfer coefficient, J, will decrease the required overlay thickness by approximately 1.0 in.” This reduction can bring the design down to the practical minimum; however, a project constraint limits the use of dowels: “dowels are not recommended for overlay thicknesses less than 7 in.” The minimum seven‑inch thickness is required to provide adequate concrete cover over the bars and avoid interference from vibrators during placement, as reflected in the statement “This change allows reducing the overlay thickness to 7 in.” [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 48–52)

Design methods and criteria

Overlay Thickness Optimization is executed as a step‑by‑step design sequence that integrates traffic loading, material properties, serviceability limits, and load‑transfer mechanisms to determine the required slab thickness (D_f) and then derives the overlay thickness (D_ol). The procedure begins with the selection of a design life, which fixes the cumulative equivalent single axle loads (ESALs); these ESAL values are fed into Step 1 of the AASHTO design routine together with a load‑transfer coefficient (J) to compute D_f. The coefficient J is obtained from Table 2.6 of the 1993 AASHTO Guide and varies with shoulder type, dowel usage, or other reinforcement details. Material characteristics such as concrete modulus of rupture, elastic modulus, and subgrade reaction modulus (k‑value) are also entered in Step 1; changes in any of these parameters alter D_f through the underlying empirical relationships embedded in the AASHTO slab‑design equations.

Once D_f is established, an effective existing slab depth (D_eff) is calculated based on the condition of the underlying pavement and its reinforcement. The overlay thickness is then obtained by either a direct subtraction or a geometric relationship: - Linear form: D_ol = D_f – D_eff, - Square‑root form: D_ol = √(D_f² – D_eff²). Both expressions are applied iteratively; if the resulting D_ol falls below practical limits (e.g., 7 in for doweled overlays to ensure adequate concrete cover and avoid vibration‑induced bar displacement), the design is adjusted by modifying J, dowel geometry, or design life until an acceptable thickness is achieved. The process may be repeated with revised ESALs when the assumed design life is shortened or extended, producing a corresponding increase or decrease in D_f and thus D_ol. Minimum overlay thickness requirements and reinforcement spacing (e.g., 1.5 in dowels at 12 in centers) are incorporated as empirical constraints within the iterative loop. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 37–65)

Overlay thickness optimization is governed by a set of deterministic design criteria that link assumed service life, material properties, and load‑transfer mechanisms to a minimum required thickness. The primary assumption is that cumulative traffic loading (ESALs) is dictated directly by the chosen design life; any extension of design life raises ESAL exposure and therefore the slab thickness needed for structural adequacy. Material assumptions include a concrete modulus of rupture of 650 psi and an elastic modulus of 4,100,000 psi, which affect the calculated slab thickness. Load‑transfer is represented by the coefficient J; incorporating dowels reduces J from 3.8 to 2.8, decreasing required thickness but imposing a hard lower bound of seven inches to guarantee sufficient concrete cover and avoid interference with vibrators. The optimization therefore applies the following thresholds: (1) a minimum overlay thickness of 7 in when dowels are used; (2) each increase in ESALs or material strength that pushes the calculated slab thickness past the next integer value results in an approximately one‑inch increase in rounded overlay thickness; and (3) extending design life from 20 to 40 years adds roughly two inches to the minimum overlay. These criteria act as implicit factors of safety, ensuring that the overlay retains adequate structural capacity over its intended service period. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 37–62)

Structural section and dimensions

Overlay Thickness Optimization specifies only slab/overlay dimensions; it does not require any base, subbase, treated‑soil, pavement, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement layer thicknesses beyond the slab itself. For a design life of 30 years the calculated slab thickness rises to about 12.2 in., which yields an overlay thickness of roughly 4.8 in. and is rounded to 5.0 in. When reinforcement is provided by 1.5‑inch diameter dowels on 12‑inch centers, concrete cover considerations set a practical minimum overlay thickness of 7 in. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 37–65)

Materials and mixture design

Overlay Thickness Optimization must specify (1) the existing pavement concrete strength—its modulus of rupture and elastic modulus—as these properties directly drive slab‑depth calculations; for example a concrete with a “modulus of rupture of 650 psi” and an “elastic modulus of 4,100,000 psi” raises the required slab thickness. (2) the dynamic modulus of subgrade reaction (k‑value), because changing this value alters the computed slab depth—“dynamic modulus of subgrade reaction is changed from 1,000 to 500 psi/in”. (3) the shoulder material type, which sets the load‑transfer coefficient used in thickness equations; “If asphalt shoulders were used instead of tied concrete shoulders, the load transfer coefficient would change from 2.8 to 3.2.” These three material specifications—concrete strength parameters, subgrade k‑value, and shoulder material/associated load‑transfer coefficient—must be defined for any overlay thickness optimization. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 37–65)

Structural details and interfaces

Overlay Thickness Optimization requires a load‑transfer system that incorporates dowel bars as the primary reinforcement at joints. The design example calls for 1.5 in diameter dowels placed on a 12 in spacing, which permits the overlay to be reduced to about seven inches. Dowel bars are only suitable when the final overlay thickness is ≥7 in because sufficient concrete cover must be maintained and concrete vibration can otherwise damage the bars. When dowels are used together with tied concrete shoulders, the load‑transfer coefficient J drops from 3.8 to 2.8, yielding roughly a one‑inch reduction in required overlay thickness. Conversely, selecting asphalt shoulders instead of tied concrete shoulders raises the coefficient from 2.8 to 3.2 and adds approximately one inch to the needed overlay thickness. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 48–65)

When designing the interfaces between pavement layers, the use of dowels together with tied concrete shoulders is prescribed to improve load‑transfer efficiency across an unbonded overlay; this lowers the load‑transfer coefficient from 3.8 to 2.8 and consequently reduces the required slab thickness. The guidance calls for 1.5 in. dowels placed at 12 in. centers, which permits reducing the overlay thickness to about 7 in. However, practical limits dictate that overlays thinner than this are avoided because they would not provide enough concrete cover over the bars and could be compromised by concrete‑placement vibrators. Choosing an asphalt shoulder instead of a tied‑concrete shoulder raises the load‑transfer coefficient from 2.8 to 3.2, which increases the calculated slab thickness from 12.4 in to 13.4 in and consequently adds about one inch to the required overlay thickness, changing it from roughly 9.5 in to 10.5 in. [1]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Overlay thickness optimization must incorporate a safety‑driven geometric constraint when dowels are employed for load transfer: the overlay shall not be thinner than approximately seven inches. This lower limit guarantees sufficient concrete cover over the reinforcement bars and prevents concrete‑vibrating equipment from striking the dowels, thereby preserving structural integrity and protecting construction personnel. [1]

[1] — 1. Introduction (pp. 48–52)

Pavement Maintenance

Part of: Joint Sealing is-subtopic-of
Pavement Maintenance is a topic under Joint Sealing.

Created 2026-09-01 · Updated 2026-09-02

Pavement maintenance comprises a set of techniques designed to stabilize concrete slabs and preserve the integrity of joints in existing roadways. Slab-stabilization injection involves pumping materials beneath the slab to fill voids, bond the pavement to the subgrade, and prevent water infiltration [1]. Specific methods such as polyurethane injection utilize expanding foam that adheres to concrete under controlled conditions, while asphalt undersealing employs hot liquid asphalt pumped beneath joints to create a moisture-blocking bond [1]. These interventions require the applied materials to cool or cure before traffic is permitted to resume, ensuring the protective coating remains intact and is not damaged by vehicle contact [1].

Joint Sealing and Slab Stabilization Techniques

Joint sealing and slab stabilization are maintenance techniques designed to preserve the structural integrity of concrete pavements by preventing water infiltration and restoring support beneath slabs [1]. Slab stabilization involves injecting materials such as polyurethane or asphalt beneath the pavement to fill voids, bond the slab to the subgrade, and prevent further deterioration caused by moisture loss [1]. Proper application requires controlled mixing and pumping of these materials, followed by adequate curing or cooling time before traffic can resume to ensure the protective layer remains intact [1].

State of the art — Current best practices for slab stabilization prioritize polyurethane injection using dedicated pumping units to achieve rapid restoration, allowing traffic resumption in as little as 15 to 30 minutes [1]. Hot-asphalt undersealing remains a widely used alternative that requires strict safety protocols and keeps roadways closed for at least 30 minutes until the material solidifies [1]. Joint restoration relies on mature formed-in-place hot-pour sealant technologies where installation follows precise temperature and agitation guidelines, with traffic prohibited until the sealant is tack-free [1].

Findings from the reports

  • Found that sealing diamond-ground and grooved pavement surfaces requires keeping them closed to traffic until the sealant has fully cooled or cured, as premature opening causes the material to be picked up by vehicle tires [1].

Novelty & rigor — The approach introduces specialized injection and thermal systems that utilize dedicated pumping equipment with precise pressure and temperature controls to ensure proper material mixing and delivery [1]. Reproducibility is achieved through strict adherence to procedural specifications, including the use of indirectly heated double-boiler melters with active agitation and thermostatic limits for sealant application [1].

Implications — Maintenance protocols must enforce a mandatory closure period following joint sealing operations to allow the sealant to fully cure, thereby preventing premature traffic damage and ensuring the longevity of the treatment [1].

Limitations — The effectiveness of the sealant is compromised if traffic access is granted before the material has fully cooled or cured, as premature exposure risks lifting the protective layer [1]. This curing requirement imposes a practical constraint on the speed at which repaired pavement sections can be returned to active service [1].

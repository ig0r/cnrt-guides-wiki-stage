Life-Cycle Assessment of Environmental Impact

life-cycle-assessmentpavement-sustainabilityenvironmental-impactlca-toolssustainability-evaluation
Created 2026-09-09 · Updated 2026-09-09

Life‑cycle assessment (LCA) of environmental impact is a systematic, quantitative technique for analyzing and comparing the full suite of material, energy, emission and waste flows associated with a pavement product, system or process from raw‑material extraction through construction, operation, maintenance and end‑of‑life. Its primary purpose is to support decision makers in selecting designs, materials or preservation treatments that minimize resource use, greenhouse‑gas emissions, waste and other adverse impacts over the entire service life, thereby avoiding burden shifting between life‑cycle stages. By defining a clear goal and scope, compiling an inventory of all relevant flows, applying impact‑assessment methods consistent with ISO 14040/44, and interpreting the results, LCA provides an objective, repeatable comparison of total environmental burdens across alternatives. The approach is applicable to new construction, rehabilitation, retrofit and disposal decisions, and its credibility rests on meeting defined quality dimensions of accuracy, currency and completeness.[1][2][3]

Purpose and decision context

Life‑cycle assessment (LCA) is used to inform the choice of a pavement alternative by quantifying the environmental impacts associated with each material, design or maintenance option over its entire service life, thereby helping decision makers avoid solutions that shift burden from one phase to another. Life‑Cycle Assessment is employed to support the choice of pavement designs or materials by quantitatively comparing the environmental impacts of competing alternatives so that agencies can select the option that minimizes resource use, emissions and waste over the pavement’s entire service life. Life‑cycle assessment is intended to support the decision of which pavement design or material to select by providing an objective, repeatable comparison of total environmental impacts across all life‑cycle stages. Life-cycle assessment is intended to support the selection of a pavement preservation or rehabilitation treatment by quantifying and comparing the environmental consequences of each alternative over its entire service life. [1][2][3][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 27–30); [2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–26); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–106), Ch. 11 (p. 111); [4] — Ch. 12 (p. 275)

Life‑Cycle Assessment is employed to resolve the absence of a systematic, quantitative framework that evaluates the full environmental burdens of pavement construction, maintenance, and preservation across all life‑cycle stages. By providing a technique to analyze and quantify material, energy, and emission flows from raw‑material extraction through end‑of‑life, LCA enables planners and engineers to compare design and material alternatives, balance initial versus future economic and environmental costs, and avoid hidden impacts that single‑phase rating systems miss. The overarching performance goal is to supply a comprehensive, comparable data set that supports decision‑making aimed at reducing the overall environmental impact of pavement systems over their service life. [1][2][3][4]

Scope and applicability

The guides agree that Life‑cycle assessment (LCA) is appropriate for essentially all pavement projects whenever a full‑scope view of environmental impacts is required. It can be applied to new construction, rehabilitation or retrofit work on existing assets, and end‑of‑life decisions such as removal, recycling or reuse. The use of LCA in pavement projects provides a quantitative approach for comparing the environmental impacts of competing alternatives and can assist in making decisions that lower the environmental impact of a pavement over its life cycle. Using this pavementspecific LCA framework, environmental impacts over the life cycle can be quantified, adverse environmental impacts can be identified and avoided, and progress towards a more sustainable infrastructure can be monitored. Applying LCA during the treatment‑selection phase enables agencies to compare the environmental footprints—such as greenhouse‑gas emissions, ozone depletion, and smog formation—of different preservation or rehabilitation strategies. [2][3][4]

The guides note that “Life‑cycle assessment (LCA) is a technique that can be used to analyze and quantify the environmental impacts of a product, system, or process.” However, “The International Standards Organization provides overarching guidance for LCA, but specific standards for use with pavements are still being developed,” which leads to situations where “pavement LCA results must be carefully scrutinized because their data sources and system boundaries (i.e., what processes are and are not considered) tend to vary between individual tools and studies.” Consequently, “Life‑cycle assessment can quantify a pavement’s environmental impacts, but it is less appropriate when the necessary data are incomplete or inconsistent and when system boundaries cannot be clearly defined because pavement‑specific standards are still being finalized; under those conditions the results must be scrutinized and other tools—such as performance assessment for functional criteria, life‑cycle cost analysis for economic evaluation, or a sustainability rating system that aggregates best practices—are preferable.” The guides also warn that “These tools should be used primarily to help guide users; they should not be used for material selection,” and that “It becomes inappropriate when the decision at hand involves material selection because LCA does not capture the performance or cost trade‑offs that drive such choices; a dedicated material‑selection framework or multi‑criteria decision analysis is required instead.” When an appraisal must integrate economic and societal (equity) considerations, “LCA alone is insufficient—analysts should pair it with life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) and emerging equity‑focused tools, or adopt a broader multi‑criteria methodology.” Moreover, “Some of the tools reviewed are still undergoing development and are thus likely to change in the coming year,” and “No one tool is currently available that simultaneously considers economic, environmental, and equity (societal) impacts,” indicating that alternative rating systems or complementary analyses should be employed when mature LCA tools are unavailable. [1][3]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 27–28); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–106), Ch. 11 (p. 111)

Information needs

The guides concur that a pavement LCA starts with a clear goal and scope that defines the functional unit, system boundaries and the alternatives to be compared. The inventory phase then requires quantitative data on all material quantities, energy consumption, air‑, water‑ and soil‑emissions, and waste generated throughout construction, use, maintenance and end‑of‑life, typically drawn from up‑to‑date life‑cycle inventories or environmental product declarations. To satisfy this requirement the analyst must gather data on every stage of the product’s life: quantities of raw material extraction and associated fuel use, manufacturing inputs for each component, transport distances and energy for moving materials, details of assembly and construction activities, and operational information such as maintenance schedules, repair frequencies, lighting energy consumption and traffic‑related emissions. Site‑specific conditions—such as the availability of recycled concrete on‑site versus imported aggregates—must be recorded because they affect transportation impacts. In addition, a comprehensive LCA must capture site climate influences, existing pavement condition, traffic volumes, and material and construction costs so that economic information can be incorporated and stakeholder input can shape the selected impact categories. [2][3][4]

[2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–25); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 97–105); [4] — Ch. 12 (p. 275)

Across the guides, a pavement LCA is required to meet three interrelated quality dimensions—accuracy, currency, and completeness.

Accuracy: The information must be “Information used for a pavement LCA must be sufficiently accurate to reflect true material, energy, emissions and waste flows,” and “data should satisfy the quantitative criteria of ISO‑compatible LCA standards and faithfully represent the true mass, energy and environmental flows that drive the impact results.”

Currency: It is essential that “it is important to verify that the tool is maintained and that the associated databases are current,” and practitioners are urged to “practitioners should use the most up‑to‑date versions and datasets available to capture recent methodological advances and material innovations.”

Completeness: The inventory must “impact categories in LCA are typically defined as energy or resource usage, emissions, toxicity, water, and waste” and also “address the full suite of relevant impact categories (energy use, land use, climate change, health effects, acidification, toxicity, etc.).” Moreover, it should “the inventory should span the full cradle‑to‑grave chain—raw‑material extraction, manufacturing, transport, construction, operation, maintenance, and end‑of‑life handling.” While minor upstream inputs below a 1–5 % threshold may be excluded, “the overall dataset must be transparent, repeatable, and sufficiently comprehensive to support robust comparison of alternative pavement designs and verification of sustainability claims.”

These combined requirements ensure that LCA results are reliable for assessing the environmental impacts of pavement construction and maintenance. [2][3]

[2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–25); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–105), Ch. 11 (p. 111)

Alternatives

Applying life-cycle assessment to pavement projects requires comparing a range of feasible options that cover design configurations, material and structural choices, preservation treatments, and the full suite of life‑cycle stages. The guides advise examining ‘selection of design types (such as continuously reinforced concrete pavement [CRCP]) and design elements (e.g., thicker slabs, stronger bases, better load-transfer devices, good drainage)’. Surface‑renewal options are highlighted by noting ‘smoothness to be easily and repeatedly restored through a surface renewal preservation technique like diamond grinding’, while low‑volume alternatives include ‘thin pavements with short joint spacing or concrete pavers’. End‑of‑life pathways should also be considered, as reflected in the recommendation of ‘End‑of‑life alternatives or use of extremely longlived pavement, which will not be expected to need reconstruction over a period of 50 years or more’.

Across the life‑cycle, the stages that can be compared are listed as ‘Extraction of materials and fuel used for energy’, ‘Manufacture of components’, ‘Transportation of materials and components’, ‘Assembly and construction’, ‘Operation, including maintenance, repair, and user impacts such as energy for lighting and traffic emissions’, and ‘Demolition, disposal, recycling, and reuse of the pavement at the end of its functional or useful life’. In addition, practitioners should ‘compare alternative pavement designs’ and may evaluate specific design alternatives such as those illustrated by ‘Alternative No. 2, which is the as‑built two‑lift pavement section, had a number of innovative features beyond the use of the two‑lift design.’ The overarching purpose is that ‘Environmental impacts can be assessed using an LCA’.

When focusing on preservation and rehabilitation, the guides note that ‘Life-cycle assessment (LCA) is a technique used to analyze the environmental impacts associated with a product, system, or process.’ An analysis can ‘determine and compare the environmental impacts associated with various pavement preservation and rehabilitation strategies, such as energy use, greenhouse gas emissions, ozone depletion, and smog formation, among others’. Options that tend to lower impact include ‘longerlasting treatments (by virtue of their design or good construction quality), thinner treatments, and those that have the greatest impact on preserving ride quality and surface characteristics are noted to have a reduced environmental impact over the pavement life cycle.’ [1][3][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 29–30); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 97–106); [4] — Ch. 12 (p. 275)

Decision criteria

Across the guides, sustainability is identified as the controlling criterion for any life‑cycle assessment of pavement environmental impact. The guides state that decisions should be driven chiefly by sustainability – specifically, minimizing the pavement’s environmental impact over its entire service life. Sustainability, expressed as the magnitude of environmental impacts across the life cycle, therefore takes precedence over other considerations.

While sustainability dominates, the IMCP manual notes that performance (smoothness, texture and structural response that affect fuel use and emissions), cost (the trade‑off between higher upfront expenditures for longer‑lasting designs and future economic and environmental costs of more frequent maintenance) and durability/longevity also must be weighed in the LCA analysis. The RCA guide adds that cost or societal effects may be considered as secondary information, but the primary control remains the reduction of adverse environmental outcomes.

Conversely, none of the sources list safety, schedule, accessibility or broader public impact as controlling criteria for the LCA decision; these factors are omitted from the boundary‑setting process. [1][2][3]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 29–30); [2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–25); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–106), Ch. 11 (p. 111)

When evaluating competing environmental‑impact criteria in a pavement LCA, analysts should first focus on material flows that constitute a substantial share of the product’s mass and that drive notable toxic emissions or energy use. Inputs that fall below a small‑percentage threshold, lack significant toxicity, and have minimal energy impact can be de‑emphasized or omitted, keeping the inventory manageable. Impact categories (e.g., climate change, acidification, ecotoxicity) are normally presented side‑by‑side rather than collapsed into a single weighted score. Although weighting is technically possible during the LCIA phase, ISO 14044 restricts its use for public comparative studies; therefore weights should only be applied in internal decision‑making when a specific, justified framework exists. [3]

[3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 98–99)

Analysis methods and tools

The evaluation framework for pavement life‑cycle assessment combines several complementary tools. Performance assessment is described as “considering performance through assessment of specified physical attributes deemed necessary for a pavement to meet its intended function,” with “condition ratings, pavement structural capacity, pavement ride quality, and frictional characteristics” cited as the key indicators of serviceability. Economic impacts are addressed by “life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) … evaluating the total cost of an investment option over its entire life.” Environmental impacts are quantified through “life‑cycle assessment (LCA) … to analyze and quantify the environmental impacts of a product, system, or process.” Quantitative LCA modeling relies on general‑purpose software such as Athena, SimaPro and EPA’s TRACI, together with pavement‑specific packages like PaLATE, BE2ST‑in‑Highways and emerging platforms such as the Illinois Tollway LCA, all operating under the FHWA Pavement Life‑Cycle Assessment Framework. The results of LCA (and associated LCCA) are then fed into formal sustainability rating systems—INVEST, Greenroads, Envision and GreenLITES—which “provide structured criteria, scoring methods and credit points that enable self‑evaluation and certification across planning, design, construction, operation and maintenance phases.” Rating systems are further described as “essentially lists of sustainability best practices with an associated common metric” for broadly comparing alternatives. While traditional pavement evaluation instruments such as decision trees, risk assessment or benefit‑cost analysis are noted as not specified in the current guidance, the combined use of performance metrics, LCCA, LCA software, and rating systems constitutes the primary suite of tools employed across the guides. [1][2][3]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 27–30); [2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–26); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 96–106), Ch. 11 (p. 111)

The reliability of pavement life-cycle assessment hinges on a series of explicit and implicit assumptions that shape every phase of the study. First, the definition of goal and scope determines which processes are counted; consequently “their data sources and system boundaries (i.e., what processes are and are not considered) tend to vary between individual tools and studies.” Because pavement-specific standards are still emerging, analysts must choose among different methodological options, each introducing its own degree of uncertainty.

A recurring source of uncertainty is the treatment of recycled materials. “Key to consideration of recycled concrete in an LCA is its definition as a waste or a product,” which governs whether environmental credits are applied and how double-counting is avoided.

Energy accounting further compounds variability. The guides note that “Definitions related to energy, and reporting of energy use in LCA, are controversial and thus are typically reported in terms of primary energy and feedstock energy,” meaning that the same inventory can yield divergent energy-use results depending on the chosen definition.

Assumptions about pavement performance—such as longer-lasting, thinner treatments delivering lower impacts—are also highly context-dependent. “These relative comparisons, however, are very broad and may vary considerably depending on the prevailing traffic levels, climatic effects, pavement conditions, and material and construction costs associated with each treatment.”

Additional uncertainties arise from cut-off thresholds for low-mass or low-energy inputs, omission of upstream processes (e.g., plant construction, heating/cooling), selection of impact categories (midpoint versus endpoint), and the limited availability of high-quality data. Together, these assumptions and data gaps create a range of possible outcomes that must be interpreted as broad, context-specific estimates rather than precise predictions. [1][2][3][4]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 27–28); [2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–25); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–106); [4] — Ch. 12 (p. 275)

Stakeholders and constraints

During the goal‑and‑scope stage the team that conducts the LCA supplies the necessary input by establishing cut‑off criteria for what material and energy flows are included, selecting the impact categories to be examined, and identifying the data types and sources required. Owners, agency officials, consultants, contractors and specifiers are the parties that must supply the necessary data and judgments for a pavement life‑cycle assessment; they evaluate sustainability claims and feed those inputs into the chosen LCA tool. Ultimately, transportation agencies are expected to adopt the findings—once user‑friendly tools are available—to approve or implement the selected pavement design and material options. If a review is called for, approval of the LCA results comes from that external reviewer. [3]

[3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 97–105), Ch. 11 (p. 111)

The choice of a life‑cycle assessment (LCA) methodology for pavement projects is bounded primarily by established standards rather than specific permits or funding rules. “The International Standards Organization provides overarching guidance for LCA, but specific standards for use with pavements are still being developed,” and the approach must also be “standardized by the International Standardization Organization (ISO) under ISO 14040 and ISO 14044.” ISO 14044 together with guidance from SETAC and the U.S. EPA defines the required goal‑and‑scope definition, inventory analysis, impact assessment, and interpretation steps that must be followed, while ASHRAE 189.1 mandates that an LCA report must present a defined set of impact indicators such as acidification, climate change and human‑health effects and “According to ISO 14044, weighting “shall not be used in LCA studies intended to be used in comparative assertions intended to be disclosed to the public.”” Federal guidance further constrains practice: “Guidelines are currently under development by the FHWA to address current gaps with regard to pavement LCA,” and “the FHWA has recently supported development of a Pavement Life‑Cycle Assessment Framework, which provides guidance tailored to the pavement community on the LCA approach, methodology, and system boundaries.” Sustainable‑highway rating systems also shape acceptable methods; “The FHWA’s INVEST (FHWA 2012a) and Greenroads … are two examples of mature sustainable highway rating systems that include pavements,” and the Greenroads™ program requires the project team to complete the PaLATE Version 2.0 software tool and report total energy use and CO₂‑equivalent global warming potential (other tools may be used only if they provide the same transparent outputs). Design requirements must align with existing highway design standards, specifically the “1993 American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials [AASHTO] Guide for Design of Pavement Structures” or its mechanistic‑empirical implementation such as “ME design (e.g., AASHTOWare Pavement ME Design),” and Greenroads sets thickness thresholds tied to ESAL ranges that determine eligibility for long‑life pavement credits, e.g., “for a lifetime equivalent single‑axle load (ESAL) less than 500,000, the Greenroads™ program says that a 7-in. thick concrete pavement is equivalent to a 6-in. thick hot‑mix asphalt (HMA) pavement.” Product category rules and environmental product declarations are limited to those that satisfy the rating‑system criteria. [1][2][3]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 27–30); [2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–25); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 97–105)

Timing and implementation

The decision to undertake a life‑cycle assessment should be made during the goal‑and‑scope definition stage, before any inventory work begins. At that point the system boundaries are set, impact categories are chosen, cut‑off thresholds for mass, energy and environmental significance are established, and the data types and sources needed for the LCI are identified, along with any requirement for an independent review. Making the decision at this early stage ensures that all subsequent analyses use consistent boundaries and criteria. [3]

[3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 97–98)

Action on LCA‑based environmental impact assessments is triggered by several clear events and thresholds. First, any update or new release of assessment tools demands immediate review; the report notes that “Some of the tools reviewed are still undergoing development and are thus likely to change in the coming year,” so practitioners must obtain the latest tool capabilities before use. Second, during the goal‑and‑scope phase, reaching predefined cut‑off thresholds for mass, energy or environmental significance—“cut‑off criteria are established for input and output to the boundary”—requires a redefinition of product boundaries and an update to the life‑cycle inventory; any deviation in boundaries or failure to meet data‑quality standards also mandates corrective action, potentially including an independent third‑party review. Third, material or input contributions that exceed small‑share limits—typically more than 1 %–5 % of total mass, embodied energy, or emissions—must be explicitly modelled; otherwise they may be omitted from the inventory. Finally, project milestones such as entering a Greenroads™ evaluation trigger LCA execution: “The project team is required to complete the PaLATE Version 2.0 software tool for the project and report the total energy use and global warming potential in CO₂‑equivalents.” Traffic‑load thresholds (e.g., ESAL < 500,000 or > 50 million) further prompt reassessment of pavement classification and eligibility for long‑life credits. [3]

[3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–105)

To conduct the chosen LCA option you will need specialized software such as SimaPro to handle the extensive material‑flow data, plus reliable life‑cycle inventories like those published by the Portland Cement Association for cement and concrete. The analysis must follow the ISO‑standard four‑step workflow—defining goals and scope, compiling a life‑cycle inventory, performing impact assessment, and interpreting results—and each step can be time‑intensive because it requires manipulating large data sets. Supporting documents from ISO, SETAC or EPA provide the procedural framework that ensures scientific transparency and repeatability. [3]

[3] — Ch. 6 (p. 97)

Outcomes and follow-up

Success for a pavement life‑cycle assessment is realized when the study adheres to the ISO‑defined four‑step process—goal and scope definition, inventory analysis, impact assessment, and interpretation—and delivers a complete accounting of all material and energy flows from raw‑material extraction through end‑of‑life. Success is achieved when the sustainable attributes of a pavement are systematically assessed and quantified so that improvements can be identified and innovation guided. The use of LCA in pavement projects provides a quantitative approach for comparing the environmental impacts of competing alternatives and can assist in making decisions that lower the environmental impact of a pavement over its life cycle. Impact categories in LCA are typically defined as energy or resource usage, emissions, toxicity, water, and waste. Rating systems promote innovation in design and construction and provide an avenue for communicating sustainability achievements. Success for a pavement life‑cycle assessment is realized when the analysis can consistently quantify all energy and material flows from raw‑material extraction through end‑of‑life and translate them into comparable impact metrics such as total energy use, greenhouse‑gas emissions, ozone depletion potential and smog formation. This success is measured by applying the FHWA’s LCA framework—or its forthcoming pavement LCA tool—to each preservation or rehabilitation alternative, gathering the required input data across the full life cycle, and reporting the standardized environmental impact results; lower values in these categories indicate a more sustainable outcome. [2][3][4]

[2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–26); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–106), Ch. 11 (p. 111); [4] — Ch. 12 (p. 275)

Monitoring of pavement LCA results must be continuous and systematic. The guides stress that the analysis follow the standard four‑phase workflow—goal and scope definition, inventory analysis, impact assessment, and interpretation—and that quantitative values for each impact category (energy or resource use, emissions, toxicity, water, waste) be recorded in a formal inventory that references its data sources.

“Using this pavementspecific LCA framework, environmental impacts over the life cycle can be quantified, adverse environmental impacts can be identified and avoided, and progress towards a more sustainable infrastructure can be monitored.” This monitoring is achieved by employing up‑to‑date LCA software (e.g., PaLATE Version 2.0) and by updating results whenever newer databases become available.

Documentation should list each endpoint impact separately—acidification, climate change, ecotoxicity, eutrophication, human‑health effects, land use, ozone depletion, resource use, smog—without weighting when the findings are intended for public comparison. The results are then normalized so that “the worst‑performing alternative at 100 %” and all others are expressed as percent reductions, enabling clear side‑by‑side comparisons.

“It is essential that the sustainable features of pavements be assessed and quantified to recognize improvements and guide innovation.” By feeding these audited, normalized results back into the LCA tools, agencies can pinpoint hotspots, refine material mixes and construction methods, and steer future design choices toward lower impacts while maintaining performance. As one source reminds us, “if something is not measured, it will not get done,” underscoring that rigorous measurement, documentation, and iterative use of LCA findings are indispensable for improving later decisions. [2][3]

[2] — Ch. 2 (pp. 24–25); [3] — Ch. 6 (pp. 95–106)
